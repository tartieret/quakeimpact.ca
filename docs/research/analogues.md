## Findings

### The discipline that governs this whole file

Every figure here is **[C]**. Each illustrates a mechanism. None of them forecasts anything
about Metro Vancouver, sets an impact band, or becomes a local number. Christchurch's sanitation
figures describe Christchurch. Berlin's tonnage describes 1949 air logistics. Where a mechanism
also has a Lower Mainland source, the local source leads and the analogue follows — that is now
the case for fuel, and it should become the case for everything else over time.

Some of the sources behind these analogues are institutional websites or professional journals
rather than government, utility or peer-reviewed publications, and are marked accordingly: a
museum, a broadcaster and a US Army logistics journal carry most of the Berlin figures, with a
USAF historical office fact sheet behind the dates and the American totals, and news media carry
the Christchurch ones.

### Christchurch 2011 — sanitation, displacement, and masonry façades

**Sanitation.** Present both timescales or neither. [CHCH-SEWER-STUFF] [CHCH-SEWER-NZH]
[CHCH-RNZ] **[C]**

- Chemical toilets were provided for about **30,000 residents**, and **1,141 portaloos** were
  delivered. [CHCH-SEWER-STUFF] **[C]**
- Sewer repairs were projected to take **more than a couple of years**; a later council estimate
  put **full network restoration at up to 30 years**. [CHCH-SEWER-STUFF] [CHCH-RNZ] **[C]**
- The 30-year figure refers to **full network restoration**, not to how long people used
  portable toilets. Publishing it alone is misleading in one direction; publishing only the
  weeks-to-months figure is misleading in the other.
- **Do not print the "40,000 chemical toilets available" figure against the 30,000 residents.**
  It is more than one toilet per person, which is almost certainly a households-versus-residents
  mismatch or two different dates read as one snapshot, and the 40,000 figure is not confirmed
  in the retrieved source anyway. **[?]** A reader will notice a ratio greater than one, and it
  will cost more than the number is worth.

**Population loss.** Christchurch's post-earthquake population loss is the displacement analogue
the housing argument wants, and it is **cited three times in the draft with no source key at
all**. [CHCH-POP] **[?]** It must not ship until sourced.

**Masonry façades — the statistic that replaces the glass-city claim.** Of the 42 deaths from
building failures other than the CTV and PGC buildings, **35 resulted from unreinforced masonry
façades or walls collapsing, 26 of them onto pedestrians or people in vehicles**. Nearly 70% of
those deaths were of people outside the buildings. [NZ-RC-V4] **[C]**

This belongs to `buildings.md`, where the City of Vancouver's own text places the falling-debris
hazard on older masonry commercial stock on Hastings Street and Kingsway. It is a masonry
finding, not a glass one, and it illustrates a mechanism the local source already states.

### Kobe 1995 — permanent economic displacement

The Port of Kobe ranked **sixth busiest container port worldwide in 1994** and had fallen to
**seventeenth by 1997**, permanently losing traffic share. [CHANG-2000] [RMS-KOBE] **[C]**

Peer-reviewed and institutionally corroborated — the strongest analogue in the set. It
illustrates **economic displacement that outlives physical repair**, not physical damage, and
should be used for exactly that. The ranking figures were not verified inside the full text of
the Chang paper during the source audit, and the RMS retrospective was not recovered, so the
pair currently rests on citation rather than on read text. **[?]** on the specific rankings.

### Tōhoku 2011 — fuel, and why it is now a sidebar

**Lead with the local sources.** PEIRS and the DCRRA both state the fuel-dependency mechanism
for British Columbia, in government voice:

> "Fuel also holds a unique position as a critical resource due to its requirement in the
> distribution of all other supplies, first responder activities, and enabling functionality of
> certain impacted facilities and infrastructure that rely on generators."

[PEIRS] **[A]** The fast-moving consumer goods network "will be disrupted. It may take weeks or
months to recover." [PEIRS] **[A]**

**The site therefore no longer needs Tōhoku to establish that fuel is the bottleneck — only to
show what the bottleneck looked like.** What follows is a clearly-marked out-of-region sidebar
and must not carry the argument.

Source: a METI / Agency for Natural Resources and Energy briefing submitted to the Japanese
Cabinet Office disaster-management council on **4 October 2011**, *Petroleum supply in the Great
East Japan Earthquake*. Government-primary, read directly rather than through media summaries.
[METI-2011] **[A]** as a Japanese government document, **[C]** for use on this site.

- **6 of Japan's 27 refineries stopped; national refining capacity fell to about 70% of
  pre-quake.** Three of the six restarted within about a week.
- **About 40% of service stations in the Tōhoku region could not operate** immediately after the
  earthquake. By 14 April, 2,889 of 3,070 branded stations (94%) were operating.
- **303 additional tanker trucks were redeployed into Tōhoku from other regions** (status as of
  16 April 2011). This is the logistics-bottleneck number.
- **About 1,450 fuel requests from hospitals and similar facilities in the first month, about
  1,380 fulfilled, roughly 15,300 kilolitres delivered.** The full emergency channel was 1,456
  supply actions totalling 16,031 kl, corroborated in English by the Cabinet Office's *White
  Paper on Disaster Management 2011*: "fuel (approx. 16,000 kilo-litres)". [JP-WPDM-11] **[C]**

**The mechanism, which is the whole reason to keep the analogue.** ANRE's own framing is that
the product existed and the means to move it did not: the Pacific-coast oil bases that were
Tōhoku's supply hub — Sendai refinery, Shiogama, Hachinohe, Kesennuma — "stopped operating and
were unable to ship existing inventory". Shiogama alone held 25,000 kl of tankage and about
5,000 kl/day of shipping capacity and is described as "the critical supply hub for the Tōhoku
region". [METI-2011] **[C]** **The fuel was there; the terminals and the trucks to move it were
not.** That is the exact shape of the Lower Mainland argument, and "collapse" is the wrong word
for it.

**Restoration timeline.** [METI-2011] **[C]** Day 3, the national stockholding obligation was
cut by three days of demand. **Day 6 (17 March), Shiogama terminal function was restored**,
reopening tanker delivery into Miyagi. Day 10, the obligation was cut a further 22 days and rail
transport of about 6,000 kl/day began to Utsunomiya. At about two weeks, fuel was moved **in
drums** to municipalities whose stations were destroyed, with temporary "mini service stations"
in nine municipalities fuelling about 8,800 vehicles between 27 March and 13 April. In early
April "disruption to petroleum supply gradually subsided (except in some areas)" — **roughly
three weeks to broad normalisation**, against **about a year** for the destroyed Sendai refinery
to resume refining.

**ANRE's stated lesson is an information failure:** there was no pre-existing system for
collecting information on which terminals were operable, what stock they held, and where the
tanker trucks were. [METI-2011] **[C]** Pair it with the DCRRA's own line that disruption in
communications makes it difficult for coordination teams to quickly assess needs.
[DCRRA-2025] **[A]**

**Corroborating physical damage, peer-reviewed:** 3,324 hazardous-materials facilities damaged
of 211,877 surveyed (1,807 by tsunami, 1,404 by shaking; 42 fires, 122 leaks). At Kesennuma, 22
of 23 above-ground oil storage tanks were washed away, releasing an estimated 11,721 kl.
[ZAMA-12] **[C]**

**Do not use:** queue lengths or litre rationing limits (media only, no primary source); a
precise barrels-per-day breakdown (the "1,485 kb/d shut" figure is CSIS, not the Japanese
government); the start date of the Japan Sea-coast rail detour (undated in the source); Sendai's
March 2012 restart date (METI says only "several months to a year"). **[?]**

### The Berlin Airlift — a ceiling on air logistics, rebuilt

**The caveat that must travel with every use of this comparison.** Roughly two-thirds of the
airlift's tonnage was **coal**, and **Berlin's own water supply was never cut**. This is
legitimate as a ceiling on what sustained air resupply has ever achieved. It is **not** a claim
that Berlin got less water than Vancouver would need.

**Withdrawn permanently: "462 days".** No source states a day count. It is arithmetic, and
contingent arithmetic: 26 June 1948 to 30 September 1949 is 462 days only if counted inclusively
from the USAF's start date. The AlliiertenMuseum's own first-flight date of **28 June 1948**
gives 460. Write the dates instead.

**Withdrawn permanently: "starting near 90 tons/day in the first week".** No source states an
opening-week tonnage; the figure is a corruption of the **80-ton American first day**,
generalised from a day to a week. Do not publish it in any form. If a buildup line is wanted,
the two defensible ones are in the table above: the first day (26 June 1948, 32 flights, 80
short tons, American only) [BERLIN-USAF] **[A]**, or — better, because it carries the story in
one line — the first day the target was met (12 August 1948, 4,724 short tons on 707 flights)
[BERLIN-AM] **[B]**.

**Also dropped:** the "roughly every 30 seconds" half of the landing interval. The 62 seconds is
the peak-day, all-airfields average and is derivable; **the 30 seconds is unsupported** (one
source's "about one every 36 seconds" counts take-offs *and* landings). **[?]**

**Cross-checks that pass, and are worth one line each because they pre-empt the reader who does
the division.** Peak-day payload 12,941 ÷ 1,398 = **9.26 short tons per flight**; whole
operation 2,326,406 ÷ 278,228 = **8.36 short tons per flight**. Both sit just under a C-54's
roughly 10-short-ton payload, with the peak day better utilised, exactly as a maximum-effort day
should look. And 2,326,406 short tons over 26 June 1948 – 30 September 1949 is **5,036 short
tons/day = 4,568 tonnes/day** sustained, landing almost exactly on the stated planning target —
the aggregates are mutually consistent, and the two inputs that previously carried **[?]**, the
tonnage and the flight count, are now **[B]** with a named source. [BERLIN-TINE] **[B]**
[BERLIN-USAF] **[A]**

**These inputs moved by about 1%, and no conclusion moved at all.** The sustained average rose
from 4,978 to 5,036 short tons/day and the per-capita sustained figure from 2.26 to 2.28 kg. Every
headline ratio below is **unchanged**: 6.6× sustained, 2.6× best day, and all four
demand-to-peak ratios. Say so when the figures are revised, because a reader who sees the inputs
change will assume the conclusions moved with them.

#### The standards, correctly quoted

Sphere Handbook 2018, WASH Standard 2.1, still current; a revision cycle opened in March 2026
targeting a new edition by 2028. [SPHERE-2018] **[A]**

- **15 litres per person per day** — for **"drinking and domestic hygiene"**, in Sphere's own
  words. The draft's "drinking, cooking and personal hygiene" is a loose paraphrase; use
  Sphere's wording. Sphere adds that this "is never a 'maximum'".
- **7.5 litres** applies specifically to **"the acute phase of a drought"**, "for a short time".
  The draft over-generalised it to an acute phase generally. Quote it with "drought" intact.
- **50 litres per person per day** "may be the minimum acceptable amount to maintain health and
  dignity" **"in an urban middle-income context"**. Metro Vancouver is squarely that, so this is
  the **most** relevant of the three figures and the draft used none of it.
- **4 litres per person per day** is **PreparedBC's own figure**, in both the Earthquake and
  Tsunami Preparedness Guide and the general emergency-kit page: "water, four litres per person
  per day, for at least two weeks, for drinking and sanitation". [PREPAREDBC] **[A]** It is not
  an invention of the draft, and a Vancouver reader will have seen it on a government page. Run
  the comparison at both 4 L and 15 L; showing both is more credible than showing only the
  larger.

#### The comparison

Daily water demand for 2,642,825 people, against the greatest single day in the history of
sustained airlift (**11,740 tonnes**):

| Standard | Source | Daily demand | vs the Berlin peak |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: |
| 4 L — PreparedBC | [PREPAREDBC] **[A]** | 10,571,300 L = **10,571 t** | **0.90×** |
| 7.5 L — Sphere, acute phase of a drought | [SPHERE-2018] **[A]** | 19,821,188 L = **19,821 t** | **1.69×** |
| 15 L — Sphere 2.1 minimum | [SPHERE-2018] **[A]** | 39,642,375 L = **39,642 t** | **3.38×** |
| 50 L — Sphere, urban middle-income | [SPHERE-2018] **[A]** | 132,141,250 L = **132,141 t** | **11.3×** |

**The acute-phase floor is the strongest line available and the draft left it uncomputed:** even
7.5 litres a day — which Sphere says is only briefly acceptable, and only in a drought — comes
to **19,821 tonnes a day, still 1.69 times the greatest single day in airlift history**. The
standard appropriate to a wealthy urban population exceeds that day by more than eleven times.

Note also that **PreparedBC's own 4 litres is the only figure in the set that falls below the
Berlin peak**, at 0.90×. That is precisely why the draft's original comparison was weak, and it
is a fact worth stating rather than hiding.

**Correction: "roughly four times more severe" is wrong, and no multiplier should replace it.**
The draft claimed the corrected comparison was "roughly four times more severe" than the
overview's version. Severity here is a demand-to-capacity ratio and *both* inputs changed: the
old ratio was 10,571 ÷ 8,000 = **1.32×** and the new one is 39,642 ÷ 11,740 = **3.38×**, a change
of 2.56×, not four. The "four" came from the demand side alone (15 ÷ 4 = 3.75) while ignoring
that the denominator also rose. **State the comparison directly instead: 3.4 times the airlift
peak, against 1.4 times before.** Multipliers of multipliers are how a document loses a reader
who checks.

#### The per-capita framing, which kills the "Vancouver is just bigger" objection

Berlin, for a city of about 2 million:

- Sustained average delivery: 4,568 tonnes/day ÷ 2,000,000 = **2.28 kg per person per day** —
  and that is *everything*: coal, flour, medicine, the lot. [BERLIN-TINE] [BERLIN-USAF] **[B]**,
  computed from the total tonnage and the dates rather than from any day count.
- Best single day: 11,740 tonnes ÷ 2,000,000 = **5.87 kg per person per day**. **[B]**

Against which Metro Vancouver's 15 litres per person per day of **water alone** is:

- **6.6× Berlin's sustained per-capita delivery**
- **2.6× Berlin's single best day, per person**

This is population-normalised, so it removes the objection that Vancouver is simply a bigger
city — the population ratio is only 1.32 (2.64M against 2.0M). The three numbers are internally
consistent: 3.38 (tonnage ratio) = 2.56 (per-capita ratio) × 1.32 (population ratio).

A local version of the same argument exists and does not need 1949 at all: Annacis Island
normally treats roughly **430 litres per person per day** of wastewater, against a Sphere
emergency minimum of 15 — a factor of about 29. See `systems/`.

## For the page

**Mechanism sentences.** Christchurch: sanitation is the system that fails quietly and stays
failed, and the timescale for a network is not the timescale for a household. Kobe: a port that
stops does not simply restart — customers move, and some do not come back. Tōhoku: the fuel
existed; what failed was the terminals and the trucks that move it. Berlin: the largest sustained
air resupply ever attempted, at its single best moment, moved less than a third of what this
region would need in water alone.

**Guards that must travel with the airlift comparison, in the same section, not in a footnote.**

- Label it explicitly as an **order-of-magnitude comparison, not a logistics plan**.
- State the coal caveat and the never-cut-water caveat in the same breath as the ratio.
- Give every Berlin figure its unit — short tons or tonnes — or convert once at the top and work
  in tonnes throughout. Never place "12,941 tons" and "11,700 tonnes" on the same screen without
  saying they are the same quantity.
- Say the counterpoint before an expert does: **nobody plans to supply a metro region by air.**
  The real levers are restoring surface corridors and reducing demand — and reducing demand means
  people leave. That is the honest bridge from the systems pages to the displacement page.
- Publish the ratio at **both 4 L and 15 L**, and name where the 4 L comes from. Concealing the
  government's own smaller figure would be the discoverable error here.
- Give the airlift's span as **dates**, 26 June 1948 to 30 September 1949, or as "fifteen
  months". Never as a day count: no source states one, and 462 is arithmetic that depends on
  which start date is chosen.
- Where a figure here has been revised since an earlier draft — the sustained average and the
  per-capita sustained figure both moved by about 1% — say that the ratios did not move. A reader
  who watches an input change will assume the conclusion changed with it.

**Which framing to lead with.** The per-capita one. "Water alone at the humanitarian minimum is
six and a half times what Berlin received of everything, per person, per day" is harder to
dismiss than any tonnage ratio, because it answers the objection — that Vancouver is simply
bigger — before it is raised. Follow it with the acute-phase floor: even the drought minimum
Sphere permits only briefly still exceeds the greatest day in airlift history.

**Do not.** Do not give the comparison a severity multiplier. Do not let a [C] figure set a band,
a restoration time or a casualty count anywhere on the site. Do not print a ratio greater than
one toilet per person. Do not ship the Christchurch population-loss claim until it has a source.
