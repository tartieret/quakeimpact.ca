## Research question

After a destructive earthquake in a city such as Vancouver, how likely are looting, interpersonal violence, conflict over scarce supplies, or wider breakdowns in public order? Which historical events are useful comparisons, and what practical conclusions are justified by the evidence?

Haiti is intentionally excluded. Its pre-disaster poverty, state capacity, infrastructure, and humanitarian context are too different from Canada to make it a useful central comparison. Japan remains useful as a high-preparedness reference point, but should not be treated as the only or most representative analogue.

## Preliminary conclusion

The best-supported conclusion is neither “nothing will happen” nor “society will collapse.”

- Widespread panic and indiscriminate violence are not the normal public response to disasters. Mutual aid, informal rescue, sharing, and cooperation are more consistently observed.
- Theft and disorder can still occur, especially where commercial premises are open or unguarded, neighbourhoods are evacuated, communications fail, and authorities or supplies are slow to arrive.
- Taking necessities from closed or abandoned shops, opportunistic theft of valuable goods, burglary of empty homes, and organized predation are distinct behaviours. Sources often collapse all of them into the word *looting*.
- Serious violence directly caused by residents fighting over food or water appears uncommon in the better-documented comparable events. Conflict around distribution, queues, fuel, and perceived unfairness is more plausible than generalized combat for supplies.
- Household and interpersonal harms may be more important than street disorder. Christchurch research and official reporting identify family violence as an adverse post-disaster pattern even while total recorded crime declined.
- Rumours of violence can produce real harm. During Katrina, unverified reports contributed to fear, armed vigilantism, and the diversion or delay of response resources.
- The social outcome depends heavily on duration, visible institutional competence, credible information, equitable distribution, existing social trust, and whether residents believe help is coming.

For Vancouver, Christchurch is a useful baseline for social behaviour, Chile 2010 is the strongest warning case, and Katrina is valuable for understanding isolation, institutional failure, rumours, racialized threat perception, and the difference between real incidents and an exaggerated “anarchy” narrative.

## Concepts that the future page should distinguish

| Term | Working definition | Why the distinction matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Survival appropriation | Taking food, water, medicine, diapers, or other necessities when ordinary purchase and aid systems are unavailable | May be reported as looting even when it is not predatory violence |
| Opportunistic theft | Taking property from damaged or unguarded shops, vehicles, or homes | Documented after several disasters, but does not imply a general social collapse |
| Burglary in evacuated areas | Targeting empty residences or businesses | Risk may persist or emerge after the immediate rescue phase |
| Conflict over distribution | Pushing, threats, fights, or attacks near aid, water, food, fuel, or transport | More sensitive to scarcity, uncertainty, crowd management, and perceptions of unfairness |
| Interpersonal or family violence | Violence within households or existing relationships | Can rise because of stress, displacement, crowding, alcohol use, loss of services, and reduced access to support |
| Vigilantism | Armed residents or groups using force in the name of property or neighbourhood protection | Fear and rumours may create violence rather than merely respond to it |
| Public panic | Irrational, contagious behaviour that substantially endangers the group | Disaster scholarship generally finds this much rarer than popular culture suggests |
| “Elite panic” | Authorities or influential groups overreacting to an assumed threat from the public | Can lead to excessive force, withheld aid, movement restrictions, or diversion of responders |

## Case study 1: Christchurch earthquakes, 2010–2011

### Why it is useful

Christchurch is one of the closest social and institutional comparisons: a developed, English-speaking city with functioning government, high social trust, modern emergency services, and severe infrastructure disruption. The February 2011 earthquake killed 185 people and caused extensive urban damage.

### What was observed

- There was limited opportunistic theft and burglary, including theft from damaged businesses and evacuated areas. Cordons and security were partly intended to prevent it.
- There is no strong evidence of widespread fighting among residents for food, water, or fuel.
- Recorded crime in Canterbury fell sharply in 2011. Contemporary New Zealand Police figures reported a 22.2% reduction in recorded crime, including decreases in assault, robbery, public-order offences, and theft. This is an annual regional figure, not a clean causal estimate of the earthquake’s effect, but it contradicts a simple collapse narrative.
- Research examining crime timing after the Canterbury earthquakes found that overall crime decreased, with domestic violence as the notable exception.
- Community response was strongly prosocial. Studies describe neighbours sharing resources, checking on one another, participating in informal rescue, and building collective efficacy. Research funded by New Zealand’s Health Research Council found that communities with stronger pre-existing connectedness and infrastructure were better able to mobilize after the earthquakes.
- Family violence calls and reported incidents increased in parts of the post-earthquake period. Exact percentages vary by time window and dataset, so a public page should avoid presenting one number as definitive.

### Interpretation

Christchurch supports the proposition that even a severely damaged modern city does not normally turn into violent competition for supplies. It also shows why “crime” is too blunt a category: aggregate crime can fall while burglary in evacuated areas and family violence become more serious concerns.

## Case study 2: Chile earthquake and tsunami, 2010

### Why it is useful

The magnitude 8.8 earthquake affected a relatively stable, upper-middle-income democracy with functioning national institutions. It is the clearest counterexample to the idea that major disorder cannot occur in a comparatively developed society. Concepción and Talcahuano are particularly relevant because of severe utility disruption, damaged communications, delayed security deployment, and uncertainty about supplies.

### What was observed

- Supermarkets and other businesses in and around Concepción were extensively looted beginning within roughly the first day.
- Reports and response assessments distinguish an initial demand for food and necessities from later theft of appliances, electronics, furniture, and other non-essential goods.
- Police initially struggled to control affected areas. Troops were deployed and curfews imposed. Contemporary accounts reported armed residents guarding neighbourhoods and businesses.
- A USGS reconnaissance report says the looting surprised both officials and social scientists. The report also notes responses by public agencies, private organizations, and NGOs.
- The event did not produce a permanent rise in criminality. A later quasi-experimental study found a lasting reduction in property crime in affected municipalities and argues that increased social cohesion was an important mechanism.
- Cross-country research using major earthquakes as natural experiments likewise finds that disasters can increase indicators of social cohesion, with the effect eroding over time.

### Interpretation

Chile shows that localized, substantial disorder can begin quickly when utilities, communications, supply access, and visible public authority fail together. It does not show that most survivors become violent or that disorder necessarily grows with time. The coexistence of acute looting and longer-term social cohesion is important.

The most plausible mechanisms are not “people become savage when hungry.” They include:

- unclear information about when food and water will arrive;
- commercial buildings becoming physically accessible;
- delayed or poorly coordinated state response;
- low initial trust in institutions or between neighbours;
- imitation once visible theft appears to go unchecked;
- fear of others, leading to defensive arming and escalation;
- perceived inequity in aid and security.

## Case study 3: Hurricane Katrina, 2005

### Why it is useful

Katrina is not an earthquake, but it is a valuable North American comparison for prolonged urban isolation, failed communications, delayed aid, mass displacement, heat, inadequate shelter, overwhelmed institutions, and severe inequality. It also provides the strongest warning about treating early media reports as established fact.

### What was observed

- Real theft occurred, including appropriation of food, water, clothing, medicine, and other necessities as well as opportunistic theft of non-essential goods.
- Some real violent crimes occurred. Armed vigilante activity in Algiers Point and police shootings, including the Danziger Bridge case, show that post-disaster violence was not purely imaginary.
- Many of the most sensational claims from the Superdome and Convention Center, including mass murder, widespread rape, infant murder, and sniper attacks, were unsupported or greatly exaggerated.
- Rumours were repeated by officials and major media outlets before verification. Disaster researchers argue that this distorted public understanding and influenced operational decisions.
- One legal analysis reports that exaggerated accounts helped prompt the diversion of approximately 1,500 police officers from rescue toward law enforcement. This number should be checked against the underlying official record before use in public copy.
- Despite extreme conditions, residents, volunteer groups, churches, community organizations, and some businesses conducted extensive rescue, shelter, distribution, and mutual-aid work.
- Race and class strongly shaped who was portrayed as dangerous, who could evacuate, who was stranded, and how armed “protection” was understood.

### Interpretation

Katrina should be presented as two simultaneous stories:

1. a real failure of infrastructure, evacuation, relief, communications, and governance that produced suffering, some theft, and some violence; and
2. a false or exaggerated narrative of universal lawlessness that itself worsened response and exposed survivors to additional danger.

It is particularly useful for a future section on rumours and threat perception. After a communications failure, residents should expect conflicting information, recycled images, unverified claims about dangerous areas, and official uncertainty. Treating every rumour as true can be as hazardous as dismissing all security risks.

## Japan as a reference point, not the baseline

Japan’s Kobe (1995) and Tōhoku (2011) disasters are regularly cited for very low levels of reported looting and strong orderly cooperation. They remain useful evidence that extreme physical destruction does not inherently produce social disorder.

However, differences relevant to Vancouver include long experience with earthquakes, regular drills, widespread hazard awareness, trusted procedures, strong local disaster organizations, and the population’s expectations about official response. Cultural explanations such as “discipline” are too vague to be actionable and can hide the institutional investments that help produce orderly behaviour.

Japan is best used to support two narrower points:

- severe scarcity and destruction do not automatically cause violence;
- preparedness, credible institutions, shared expectations, and social norms materially affect behaviour.

### What was observed after Tōhoku, 2011

Japan is not a case of no crime. Reported crime in the three affected prefectures fell
markedly in 2011, but burglary of empty homes and shops and theft of fuel from cars were
common in the first month, alongside baseless rumours, and disorder, domestic violence and
theft of valuables became common in shelters two to four months after the earthquake.
Evacuees feared crime in shelters and burglary of their homes. Volunteer crime prevention
gave people a collective goal. [JAPAN-SHELTERS-18] The crime-pattern statements rest on
Miyagi Prefectural Police leaflets and the 2012 White Paper on Police, cited by the paper
and not read here.

Social capital and trust help explain why some Kobe neighbourhoods recovered after 1995
while others stagnated. [KOBE-SOCIAL-10]

## What can reasonably be inferred for Vancouver

### Factors that make Christchurch relevant

- functioning Canadian municipal, provincial, and federal institutions;
- professional emergency services and military aid capacity;
- relatively high baseline social trust;
- dense networks of neighbourhood, nonprofit, faith, strata, and volunteer organizations;
- widespread ability to receive or relay information once partial communications recover.

### Factors that could make a Cascadia event more difficult

- damage across a very large region rather than a single city;
- simultaneous demand across Vancouver Island, the Lower Mainland, Washington, and Oregon;
- disrupted ports, roads, bridges, railways, fuel terminals, and airports;
- neighbourhoods separated by damaged bridges, liquefaction zones, landslides, or fires;
- long utility outages and sewage disruption;
- dependence on just-in-time retail and regional distribution centres;
- uncertainty about which level of government or agency can deliver supplies;
- very uneven household preparedness and housing security.

British Columbia’s current public guidance advises households to store food and water for **at least two weeks**, which is itself evidence that official planning contemplates prolonged disruption. This preparedness recommendation should not be translated into a claim that violence begins after a particular number of days. The historical record does not support a reliable “day 3 versus day 7 versus day 14” threshold.

### Most plausible public-order problems

Ordered approximately from more credible/common to less supported:

1. panic buying immediately before or after a warning/event where shops remain open;
2. arguments, queue-jumping, and localized fights at fuel, water, charging, transport, or distribution locations;
3. theft from damaged or temporarily unguarded commercial premises;
4. burglary in evacuated residential or commercial areas;
5. scams, price gouging, impersonation of officials, and theft from vulnerable residents;
6. increased family or interpersonal violence under prolonged stress and crowding;
7. armed or aggressive informal “security” based on rumours about outsiders;
8. attacks on supply vehicles or organized theft if shortages become prolonged and distribution repeatedly fails;
9. generalized violent competition for food and water across the city, which is possible but not the outcome best supported by comparable evidence.

## Implications for household guidance

These are research-derived directions for the eventual page, not drafted site instructions.

- Encourage two weeks of supplies primarily to reduce dependence on damaged systems and avoid hazardous trips, not to prepare for combat with neighbours.
- Frame knowing and coordinating with neighbours as a protective measure. Social connections improve information, welfare checks, pooled equipment, childcare, first aid, and informal security.
- Recommend discretion rather than secrecy or suspicion. Avoid advertising valuable supplies broadly, but do not imply that neighbours are the principal threat.
- Explain that fair, calm sharing can reduce conflict, while giving away essential household reserves without a plan can create later risk.
- Encourage residents to rely on verified official information and trusted local contacts, and to treat dramatic unverified stories cautiously.
- Advise avoiding crowds and distribution points that are visibly uncontrolled, returning at a managed time if immediate survival does not depend on the item.
- Separate personal safety from property defence. Avoid confrontations over replaceable property and do not pursue suspected thieves.
- Include a short domestic-violence safety note and access routes to emergency support. This risk is better supported than generalized stranger violence.
- Acknowledge that marginalized people may be misidentified as threats and that fear-driven profiling or vigilantism creates danger.
- Avoid weapons-centred preparedness. The reviewed evidence does not show that weapons are a general solution, and Katrina illustrates escalation risks from armed threat perception.

## Claims to avoid or qualify carefully

- **Avoid:** “Disasters bring out the best in everyone.”  
  Some predatory crime, interpersonal violence, discrimination, and vigilantism are real.
- **Avoid:** “Looting is a myth.”  
  Looting occurred in Chile and Katrina. The myth is that it is universal, overwhelmingly violent, or representative of most survivors.
- **Avoid:** “Crime falls after disasters.”  
  Results vary by crime type, geography, reporting capacity, evacuation, and time period.
- **Avoid:** “After X days without food, violence starts.”  
  No defensible universal threshold was found.
- **Avoid:** treating annual police statistics as a direct measure of behaviour in the first days after an event.
- **Avoid:** treating arrests, calls for service, media reports, victimization surveys, and recorded offences as interchangeable measures.
- **Avoid:** assuming Japanese outcomes are purely cultural or directly transferable to Vancouver.
- **Avoid:** interpreting the Chile case as proof that scarcity alone caused disorder.
- **Avoid:** repeating dramatic incident counts from early reporting unless corroborated by later investigations.

## Evidence limitations

1. **Measurement collapses after disasters.** Police stations, communications, transport, and reporting systems may be damaged. A fall in recorded crime can partly reflect displacement or reduced reporting.
2. **Definitions vary.** Research and journalism use *looting* for survival appropriation, burglary, opportunistic theft, and collective pillaging.
3. **Early reports are unreliable.** Communications gaps and competitive news cycles amplify rumours.
4. **Aggregate data conceal local peaks.** City-wide or annual crime may fall while one neighbourhood or short time window experiences severe disorder.
5. **Events differ in duration and geography.** Katrina involved flooding and delayed evacuation; Christchurch’s major disruption was more geographically contained; Cascadia could damage an entire region.
6. **Causal explanations are difficult.** Observed disorder may reflect pre-existing crime, inequality, policing, trust, building access, and governance as well as material scarcity.
7. **Modern social media may change rumour dynamics.** Katrina predates contemporary platforms, while cellular and internet networks may be intermittent after a Cascadia earthquake.

## Editorial stance recommended by the research

The future page should be reassuring without being naïve. Its central message should be that neighbours are more likely to be resources than threats, while practical preparation reduces exposure to the specific situations in which conflict does occur. The page should treat social trust as part of preparedness, not merely as a pleasant outcome after the earthquake.

It should also make a clear distinction between protecting people and defending property. Historical evidence supports avoiding unnecessary travel, crowds, rumours, and confrontation. It does not support presenting armed defence or suspicion of neighbours as a normal component of earthquake readiness.
