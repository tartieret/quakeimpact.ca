## Status

**Band: High in both scenarios.** The evidence supports a stronger and more specific
claim than the band alone. British Columbia retrofits its most important bridges — the
ones on routes the province says "must be kept open for emergency vehicle response
following a major earthquake" — to a standard of *not collapsing*, and the Ministry
states in the same document that it is not currently retrofitting them to *remain in
service*. Those are two different standards, and only the first has been bought. Where
crossing-level assessments exist they were mostly done and not published; the honest
line is **assessed, not published**, not *unassessed*. The George Massey Tunnel is the
one major crossing whose weakness is acknowledged in the province's own engineering
documents, and it has no fix in place before 2031.

## Findings

### The central finding: not collapsing and still working are different standards

The BC Ministry of Transportation's *Seismic Retrofit Design Criteria* says both of the
following, two sections apart in one document.

§2.1.2: "In the Lower Mainland and on Vancouver Island a system of routes have been
designated as Disaster Response Routes (DRRs). Disaster Response Routes are corridors
that **must be kept open for emergency vehicle response following a major earthquake.**
Lifeline bridges and bridges on Disaster Response Routes are being retrofitted as the
highest priority in the first phase of the retrofit program." [MOTI-SRDC-05] **[A]**

§2.2: "**Functional retrofitting requires that important (Lifeline and DRR) bridges
remain in service after the design earthquake. … The Ministry is not anticipating
functional retrofitting in the current stage of retrofitting; it may be considered in a
future stage.**" [MOTI-SRDC-05] **[A]**

DRR bridges in the current stage therefore receive Safety 2, Safety 1 or Superstructure
retrofits by seismic performance zone — collapse prevention, not post-earthquake
function. [MOTI-SRDC-05] **[A]**

The position has not changed in twenty years. The Ministry's February 2025 seismic
design supplement states that the objective of the retrofit program remains "to continue
to reduce the risk of bridge collapse", with performance equal to that of new bridges as
an "ultimate objective" approached in stages, and acknowledges "there may be aspects of
existing bridges that preclude economical achievement of the ultimate objective."
[MOTI-S6-SEC4] **[A]**

Two further sourced facts belong beside this and set its scale.

- The current retrofit policy "comprises upgrading to a single-level design event
  (475-year return period event)". New lifeline crossings are designed against a
  2475-year event. [MOTI-SRDC-05] [MOTI-S6-SEC4] **[A]**
- Retrofit service levels are defined in plain language. Safety 2 = "Limited access
  (e.g. reduced or designated lanes, emergency traffic). **It is recognized that
  approximately 24 hours may be needed to complete a post-earthquake inspection of the
  bridge.**" Safety 1 = "limited access to emergency traffic is possible within days
  following the earthquake. Public access is not expected until repairs are completed."
  Superstructure = "Access to traffic is not envisaged for a prolonged period."
  [MOTI-SRDC-05] **[A]**

Cite Volume 4 for the history and the retrofit program, not as current design law: the
Ministry's own standards index describes it as a background document from 2005 and
directs readers to Volume 1 Section 4. [MOTI-BSPM] **[A]**

### Approaches, not spans

Three Lower Mainland crossings have published ground-displacement figures at their
approaches, and one of them says outright that the approach, not the main span, was the
collapse risk. A fourth crossing — Mission, in the Fraser Valley rather than Metro
Vancouver — publishes the objective, the numbers and the built remediation together.

**Queensborough.** Golder Associates, reported in the Ministry's own final retrofit
report, calculated "differential displacements of order **150 mm** in a liquefiable zone
**between bents S3 and S8 after soil remediation**" — and, without that remediation,
"**the predicted displacements were considered large enough to cause a collapse of the
structure.**" [QB-MOTH-95] **[A]** This is the best published evidence anywhere that an
approach structure rather than a span carried the collapse risk at a Lower Mainland
crossing. The report does not name the remediation method; do not assert stone columns
or vibro-replacement.

**Golden Ears.** Approximately **0.5 m of ground displacement was calculated at the
river bank for the 2475-year event**, with pile foundations generally remaining elastic
across all design loading conditions. "In general, liquefaction was predicted to be
sporadic under the 475-year and subduction events; however, significant zones of
liquefaction were predicted for the 1000- and 2475-year events." Potentially liquefiable
sands up to 36 m thick overlie silts and clays to more than 120 m. [GEB-CGS-08] **[A]**

A **second, different Golden Ears displacement figure** is published, and it is not a
contradiction: "In spite of the above ground densification schemes, **some lateral soil
movements (up to approximately 0.3 m) at the main bridge piers are still expected under
the 2475 yr return period event** and have been incorporated into the main bridge
foundation design." [NAB-ICCHGE-08] **[A]** Both figures are 2475-year. They differ in
**location** and in **analysis**: up to ~0.3 m at the main bridge piers, after
vibro-flotation and vibro-replacement densification; ~0.5 m at the river edge, from a
separate analysis in the other paper, which does not say whether it is before or after
densification — and the riverbanks were densified too ("at the main river piers … and at
two riverbanks", NAB-ICCHGE-08 p.9). **The gap is not a measure of what densification
did.** Written without the location qualifiers they will be read as disagreeing.

**Golden Ears design levels are 475 / 1000 / 2475.** [GEB-CGS-08] **[A]** Independently
confirmed by a third source: "The project seismic design criteria and performance criteria
called for three levels of design earthquakes, namely a **475 year return period event
with essentially elastic performance and immediate access**, a **1000 year return period
event with repairable damage and limited access** and a **2475 year return period event
with no collapse but possible loss of service.**" A deterministic subduction event was
also specified for liquefaction assessment. [NAB-ICCHGE-08] **[A]** A secondary review
gives 975 [CAEE-10YR] **[A]**, but 975 is the CSA S6-14 *code* level and must not be
attributed to this bridge. Knight Street used 1000 as well [KSB-2010] **[A]** —
project-specific criteria in this era used 1000, not 975. Where the sources conflict,
prefer the project's own geotechnical engineers, and there are two of those.

**Golden Ears, duration — the clearest published statement in this evidence base of why
duration matters.** "for the **2475 year (yr) return period design event specified for
Golden Ears Bridge, near maximum kinematic and inertial forces occurred simultaneously**
while for the lower 475 yr event they did not and were essentially uncoupled. This
occurred because (i) **the 2475 yr event had strong shaking for approximately 30s compared
to 10s for the 475 yr event** and (ii) in the larger event soil liquefaction occurred at
an early stage in the ground shaking." [NAB-ICCHGE-08] **[A]** At thirty seconds the
ground failure and the structural shaking arrive together; at ten seconds they do not.
This pairs with the [CAEE-10YR] finding that lateral spread depends more on site gradient
and duration than on acceleration, and with the duration contrast in `../scenarios.md`.

**Golden Ears south approach viaduct — the ground was not improved.** "the bridge piers
were adjacent to an existing watermain making soil densification around the piers costly
and difficult. Utility relocation was considered but was costly. **Therefore in lieu of
soil densification the 2.5m diameter drilled shafts were designed to function with the
reduced soil lateral support and kinematic ground loading resulting from the liquefied
soil.**" [NAB-ICCHGE-08] **[A]** This is the same decision shape as Oak Street's glass-fibre
wrap: design the structure to survive the ground moving rather than stop the ground
moving. Two named Lower Mainland crossings, two different decades, the same trade-off,
both sourced — and here the stated reason is a watermain and a budget.

**Oak Street.** The 1990s retrofit's "primary objective being to prevent structural
collapse of any part of the bridge under a 475 year return period earthquake"; the south
approach sits on liquefiable soils; "the maximum differential pier settlement after
densification was evaluated as 100 mm". The chosen fix was to wrap the girders in glass
fibre so the bridge could bend rather than to stop the ground moving — a saving of over
$1 million against the ground-improvement alternative. [OAK-13WCEE] **[A]** Handle the
100 mm with care: it is expressly *after densification* — a "mitigated ground
densification scheme" from a study around 2000, not the 1994 design the paper records as
not built at the time — and the paper neither says that scheme was built nor ties the
figure to a return period, only to "design level earthquakes".

**Oak Street was reassessed in 2021–22** because "changes to codes and seismic hazard
models since that time have resulted in substantially higher seismic loading
requirements". Lateral-spreading ground displacements were estimated across
approximately 100 piers, and "additional retrofits were identified to meet the new
requirements". None of the numbers are public. [OAK-BASIS-22] **[B]**

**Mission Bridge — the one crossing where the whole chain is public.** *Geography first,
and it travels with every figure below: the Mission Bridge connects the District of
Mission to Abbotsford, roughly **80 km east of Vancouver**. It is a Fraser River crossing
on the **provincial disaster recovery network**, not a Metro Vancouver commuter crossing.
No Mission figure belongs in a sentence about Richmond or Delta.*

The bridge was built in 1973, is 1,050 m long, and is described by its retrofit engineers
as "a critical link in the Province's disaster recovery network". Its approach piers sit
on short timber piles, the river and riverbank piers on long steel H-piles, and the site
is underlain by potentially liquefiable Fraser River sand: "**liquefaction is the key
issue affecting the seismic performance of the bridge.**" [MISSION-CGS-18] **[A]**

Its stated performance criteria are the Ministry's §2.2 position in an engineer's own
words, at a named crossing. A "safety" level criterion was adopted, requiring "**collapse
prevention under a 475-year return period earthquake**", with a magnitude **M7 event at a
firm-ground PGA of 0.245 g** as the design earthquake. The criteria require the structure
to "guard against collapse of any part of the bridge", to "sustain damage levels which may
allow access to the bridge **in the days following** a major earthquake for emergency
vehicles", and to "**allow bridge closure for long term post-earthquake repairs. Access to
emergency vehicles or repairable damage to the bridge following the design earthquake was
considered desirable but not required for the adopted safety level retrofit.**"
[MISSION-CGS-18] **[A]** That last sentence is independent, crossing-level corroboration of
the central finding at the top of this file.

The quantified ground movements, all for the 475-year design earthquake described above:

- **South approach piers S5–S9**: "liquefaction-induced settlements of up to about **200
  mm** were predicted with differential settlements of **100 mm to 200 mm**".
  [MISSION-CGS-18] **[A]**
- **North approach piers N6–N10**: total settlement of up to **100 mm**, with
  differential settlement of 50–100 mm. "Existing north approach piers can accommodate the predicted settlements. However,
  **retrofit measures were required for south approach piers.**" [MISSION-CGS-18] **[A]**
- **Abutments**: post-earthquake stability analyses gave minimum factors of safety of
  **1.2 at the north abutment and 0.70 at the south**, "suggesting that significant
  displacements will occur at the north abutment and **very large or flow slide type of
  failure will occur at the south abutment.**" [MISSION-CGS-18] **[A]**
- **River piers**: "horizontal pile cap displacements in the range of **130 mm to 200 mm
  at pier S1 and 270 mm to 390 mm at pier S2** were predicted." [MISSION-CGS-18] **[A]**
- **River-bank slopes**: minimum factor of safety of unity for both banks, with liquefied
  soils at the south bank requiring treatment to depths exceeding 30 m. [MISSION-CGS-18]
  **[A]**

**And the fix was designed and built.** Horseshoe-shaped toe berms of compacted sand and
gravel, **10 m wide and about 4 m high** at the north abutment and **10 m wide, 3 m high
beneath the deck and 5 m high outside it** at the south, together with a densified zone
17.5 m deep at the south abutment. Recorded in the past tense: "A horseshoe-shaped toe
berm comprising compacted sand and gravel **was constructed** at both the north and south
berms." Also built: compaction grouting at south approach piers S5–S9, vibro-replacement
stone columns, timber compaction piles, and rings of 255 mm seismic drains at the approach
piers, verified with pre- and post-densification CPTs. The paper concludes: "The Mission
Bridge, built in 1973, **was upgraded to withstand the design earthquake.**"
[MISSION-CGS-18] **[A]**

Queensborough gives the collapse-risk quote; Oak Street gives the "design around it"
decision, with the ground improvement not built at the time; **Mission gives the complete
loop** — a stated objective that declines post-earthquake function, quantified approach
settlements, an abutment predicted to fail as a flow slide, a designed mitigation, and a
record that it was constructed.

A second crossing from the same retrofit practice carries the same criterion without
numbers: an unnamed 110-year-old, 1,050 m Fraser River railway bridge, for which "**a no
collapse under a 475 year return period earthquake was adopted as the seismic design
criterion for the retrofit of the bridge.**" Hazard sources named are local crustal, deep
in-slab and Cascadia interface, from Geological Survey of Canada uniform hazard spectra.
Retrofit was new large-diameter deep piles plus timber compaction piles, chosen because
the owner required unimpeded rail traffic during the work. No displacement magnitudes are
given. [RAIL-ECSMGE-19] **[A]** **Do not name this bridge**: the paper deliberately does
not, and any identification would be inference.

The province does publish what an approach is supposed to achieve. Within the "bridge
approach embankment interface zone", lifeline geotechnical systems must have 100% of
travelled lanes available after a 975-year event and **50% of travelled lanes available
after a 2475-year event, with normal service restorable within one month**.
[MOTI-S6-SEC6] **[A]** Since 2005 the retrofit criteria have required that "approach
embankments shall be considered where they form a necessary access to the structure"
[MOTI-SRDC-05] **[A]**, and the 2025 seismic supplement adds "damage to embankments and
abutments shall be evaluated" for existing bridges [MOTI-S6-SEC4] **[A]**.

### Queensborough, 1995 — the objective in the province's own words

"**The seismic safety retrofit was defined as a retrofit that prevents collapse during
the 1/475 year earthquake event, however the structure may or may not be functional
after the event.**" [QB-MOTH-95] **[A]**

The retrofit used base isolation as its primary component [CAEE-10YR] **[A]**: lead-cored
elastomeric bearings at piers N1/S1 and bearings without lead cores at N2/S2, chosen
partly because "S2 was founded on potentially liquefiable soils". Scope across three
contracts included footing overlays and steel column confinement jackets at **35
approach bents**, soil remediation between S2 and S8, and restrainers on the river
spans. [QB-MOTH-95] **[A]**

### The George Massey Tunnel

The tunnel is 630 m of immersed tube built 1957–59, with **550 m and 335 m of approaches
on the north and south ends** — the approaches together are longer than the crossing.
[GMC-TUNNEL-19] **[A]**

- The original design assumed a PGA of 0.21g and "did not consider the effects of soil
  liquefaction … as these were not well understood at the time." [GMC-TUNNEL-19] **[A]**
- A 1989 assessment found the tunnel "does not have sufficient strength to prevent
  collapse under the 475 year earthquake"; 1991 site investigation found liquefiable
  soils up to 20 m deep. [GMC-TUNNEL-19] **[A]**
- The 2001 retrofit strategy had two stages. Stage 1, structural and mechanical, was
  completed in 2006. Stage 2, ground improvement along the tunnel and approaches, was
  **cancelled**. "By 2006, the Stage 1 retrofit work was completed, while no ground
  improvement has been performed to date. As a result, the Tunnel does not have the
  level of safety intended in the original 2001 COWI study as the risk of tunnel
  floatation during the seismic event still exists." [GMC-TUNNEL-19] **[A]**
- "The Tunnel with the structural seismic retrofit and no ground improvement can meet
  the performance criteria for an earthquake with a return period of **approximately 150
  to 240 years**." And: "**The Tunnel does not meet seismic performance criteria defined
  in CSA S6-14.**" [GMC-TUNNEL-19] **[A]**
- An Emergency Road Closure System installed in 2008 detects seismic motion and closes
  the tunnel to new traffic while letting vehicles inside exit. It is "meant to limit the
  Tunnel use for seismic events greater than 275 year return period." [GMC-TUNNEL-19]
  **The memo attributes the 275 to a 2016 ministry memo and disputes it on the same page:**
  "It is not clear where reference [11] based its statement that the Tunnel could
  withstand a 275 year return period earthquake as this is not consistent with analysis
  by COWI." The 475 years is the **2001 retrofit's** objective ("to prevent the loss of
  life and restrict damage to a repairable level"), not the 1950s design, which assumed
  0.21 g and names no return period.
  **[A]**
- Lateral spreading is described at the site directly: "During a seismic event when the
  soil liquefies and loses its shear strength, soil near the banks of the Fraser River
  and Deas Slough will laterally spread towards the watercourses." [GMC-BRIDGE-19] **[A]**

Three numbers carry the whole argument with no adjectives: the design earthquake was
**475-year**; what the tunnel achieves is **150–240-year**; a new lifeline crossing is
designed for **2475-year**.

**The replacement.** $8.5 billion, up from $4.15 billion, with a federal contribution of
up to $3 billion; major construction from **2027**; opening
**September 2031**, moved from December 2030; environmental assessment expected to
conclude late 2026; a new eight-lane immersed tube. Announced July 2026 by Minister Mike
Farnworth and corroborated across four independent outlets reporting the same government
figures with a named minister and a dated announcement. [MOTI-MASSEY] **[A]**

The **"~150-year design life"** claim for the replacement was not confirmed in any
retrieved coverage. **[?]** Drop it or ship it as an open question. Neither 2026
government release mentions seismic design, design life, ground densification or stone
columns.

**The tunnel remains the region's one major crossing with a publicly acknowledged
seismic weakness and no fix in place until 2031.** [GMC-TUNNEL-19] [MOTI-MASSEY] **[A]**

### A worked example of what a confidence marker certifies

The Massey Tunnel has twice been described as seismically retrofitted in official
government communications:

- The Ministry's list of retrofitted structures, reported March 2011: Lions Gate,
  Ironworkers Memorial, Oak Street, Queensborough — and the Massey Tunnel.
  [PROGRESS-2011] **[B]**
- BC Gov News, 28 December 2016: "The Lions Gate, Oak Street, Ironworkers Memorial and
  Queensborough bridges, and the George Massey Tunnel have been seismically
  retrofitted." [BCGOV-2016-EQ] **[A]** *for the existence of the statement.*

Both are contradicted by the province's own 2019 engineering memo, which records the
ground-improvement stage as cancelled and the intended level of safety as not achieved.
[GMC-TUNNEL-19] **[A]**

A government release is **[A] for its own existence and not for an engineering fact its
own engineers contradict.** Prefer the COVI/Stantec memo. Do not use the tunnel item
from either release. The rest of both lists is not disputed here.

  [IRONWORKERS] **[B]** [BCSIMS-22] **[A]** The ~125,000 vehicles/day figure is not in

- **Lions Gate** (1938): the 762 m, 25-span north approach viaduct was retrofitted using
  a guided-uplift or "rocking" mechanism allowing bents to tip on one leg during shaking,

  [LIONSGATE] **[B]** The 1938 construction date and the 60,000 vehicles/day figure are

- **Knight Street** (1974, ~1500 m, over 100,000 vehicles a day) was treated as a

  standards" — with no return-period criteria. [BCGOV-2026-PATT] **[A]** Use this, not

### The Canada Line crosses the Fraser on a bridge

The Canada Line crosses the North Arm of the Fraser on the **North Arm Bridge**. The
Canada Line's bored tunnel is under **Cambie Street**, not under the river. The bridge is
**562 m long with a 180 m extradosed main span, 139 m side spans and 52 m transition
spans**, carrying two rail tracks and a suspended pedestrian/bikeway, and is North
America's first extradosed transit bridge. The extradosed form was chosen "to satisfy the
vertical flight clearance requirements at the river due to the nearby airport".
[NAB-ICCHGE-08] **[A]** These figures come from the bridge's own designer, Buckland &
Taylor Ltd., in a peer-reviewed conference paper. The Canada Line entered service on
**17 August 2009**: the Office of the Premier's news release of that date states
the line "was officially opened three-and-a-half months ahead of schedule today", and
describes it as running on "16 stations, **two bridges**, approximately 19 kilometres of
tunnel and elevated guideway". [BCGOV-CANLINE-09] **[A]** One editorial guard: the release
dates the **line**, not the bridge, and does not name the North Arm Bridge. The bridge
carries the line and had no service before it, so the date is the bridge's — but write it
as the Canada Line opening rather than implying a separate bridge-opening record. The paper
predates the opening and says only "scheduled for completion in 2009".

**Its seismic design criteria are published.** Stated by the designer: "The project
seismic design criteria state that seismic design will be based on the bridge structure
resisting a **475 year return period event with repairable damage** and a **100 year
return period event with no significant damage and essentially elastic performance.**" The paper names no other design event.
[NAB-ICCHGE-08] **[A]**

*These are the project's own criteria as reported by the bridge's designer in 2008.* The
same paper describes Golden Ears, designed in the same decade, to 475/1000/2475 — so the
contrast between two crossings is real and sourced. But the paper draws no comparison and
makes no adverse judgement, and neither may this page. **Do not write "would fail",
"under-designed", or any comparison to CSA S6-14 for this bridge.** None of that is
sourced. What is sourced is that project-specific criteria differ between owners and
structures, and that here are two published examples.

**Ground conditions.** "Soil conditions at the river include **loose to compact silty sand
and clayey silt over hard or very dense glacial till-like soils.** Depth to the top of till
decreases towards north, approximately **25 m depth at main pier S1 and 16 m depth at main
pier N1**." [NAB-ICCHGE-08] **[A]**

**Foundations.** 2 m diameter open-ended steel pipe piles driven into till at the main
piers — ten at N1, six of them battered transversely to resist design ship impact, and
nine vertical piles at S1 inside an artificial island; 2 m piles at land pier S2; 0.914 m
piles, mostly battered, at N2, N3 and S3. [NAB-ICCHGE-08] **[A]**

**The ground was deliberately not improved**, and the designer gives the reasoning: the
concrete-filled pipe piles "were designed to structurally resist both seismic inertial
forces … and kinematic soil-pile interactive forces **caused by large riverbank
displacements towards the river channel following soil liquefaction in the upper sandy
layers. No ground densification was used** as the dynamic seismic analyses … determined
that the effect of ground densification did not have much impact on the dynamic response
of the bridge. The large piled foundations embedded in dense till were very stiff both
laterally and rotationally, **even after losing some of their lateral support due to
partial soil liquefaction.**" [NAB-ICCHGE-08] **[A]** The piles were designed to resist the
loads liquefaction imposes, not to prevent the liquefaction.

"**Only partial soil liquefaction is expected at the North Arm Bridge site due to the
lower seismic motion intensity of the 475 year design seismic event.**" [NAB-ICCHGE-08]
**[A]** No ground-displacement magnitude is given for this site in that paper. **[A]** for
the absence within it.

**One bridge, one river, two different ground motions at either end.** Near-surface design
spectra "are affected significantly by the depth to the top of till varying from about 10
m at Piers N2/N3 to more than 35 m at Piers S2/S3. On the north side of the bridge where
the soil columns are shallower, the seismic motions are amplified at shorter periods less
than 0.6 second to nearly twice as much as the seismic motions on the south side."
[NAB-ICCHGE-08] **[A]**

### The most useful transit fact on this page is an event, not a standard

On **29 December 2015 at 23:39 PST** a **magnitude 4.8** earthquake occurred 12 km
southeast of North Saanich at a depth of **52.4 km**. [USGS-2015EQ] **[A]**

TransLink's own written statement: "**Although SkyTrain has been designed to withstand
seismic events, the earthquake triggered guideway intrusion alarms along the Expo and
Millennium lines.** In the interest of the public's safety, SkyTrain service was
suspended and inspections of both lines were conducted." Service resumed by 1 a.m. —
roughly **80 minutes**. "Canada Line continued to run unaffected. No intrusion alarms
were triggered by the earthquake, likely because 70 per cent of the track is
underground." [CBC-2015] **[B]**

No damage was reported. The stoppage was triggered by intrusion alarms, not by measured
damage. The reporting outlet's own gloss that the Canada Line was unaffected because it
is newer is the outlet's characterisation, not TransLink's — TransLink attributed it to
the track being underground.

### The scale of the network, and what is instrumented

"MoTI is responsible for **over 400 km of Provincial Disaster Response Routes** and
maintains **over 2500 bridges** and other infrastructure located in the highest seismic
zones of BC. The loss of any portion of these routes or infrastructure after a major
earthquake could significantly impact emergency response efforts, affect public
well-being, and hinder the economic recovery of the region." [BCSIMS-22] **[A]**

The province instruments a small fraction of that. The BC Smart Infrastructure
Monitoring System has run since 2009, with a strong-ground-motion network of over 120
earthquake sensors and structural health monitoring on **14 MoTI bridges and 1 tunnel**.
[BCSIMS-22] **[A]** The stated reason is the useful part: "**There will be many
slight-to-moderately damaged bridges after an earthquake and the ability to quickly
determine their safety will allow inspectors to focus on higher priority structures.**"
[BCSIMS-22] **[A]**

Instrumented Lower Mainland structures include the George Massey Tunnel, Queensborough,
Pitt River, Ironworkers Memorial and Port Mann, with Lions Gate and Pattullo listed as
future. [BCSIMS-22] **[A]** The source PDF's table columns extract mis-aligned; do not
pair a year or a length with a named bridge from it without re-reading the original.

## For the page

**The mechanism sentence.** The province designates routes that must stay open for
emergency vehicles after a major earthquake, and states in the same document that it is
not currently retrofitting the bridges on those routes to remain in service. Retrofits
target collapse prevention at a 475-year event; new lifeline crossings are designed for
2475. Not collapsing and still working are different standards, and only the first has
been bought.

**The central claim has crossing-level corroboration in an engineer's own words.**
The Ministry says it at policy level in §2.2. The Mission Bridge retrofit says it at one
named crossing on the provincial disaster recovery network: post-earthquake access and
repairable damage were "considered desirable but not required". [MISSION-CGS-18] **[A]**
That pairing — the policy and one crossing built to it — is stronger than either alone.
Carry the geography guard with it every time.

**The duration finding belongs on the page, and it connects to `../scenarios.md`.** At
Golden Ears, the 2475-year event shakes strongly for about thirty seconds against about
ten for the 475-year event, and at thirty seconds the ground failure and the structural
shaking peak together because liquefaction begins early in the shaking. At ten seconds
they are essentially uncoupled. [NAB-ICCHGE-08] **[A]** This is the clearest published
statement in this evidence base of why the length of the shaking, not only its strength,
decides what a crossing does.

**Lead with the pairing, not with an adjective.** Two quotations from one ministry
document, four pages apart, do the work. Present them together and stop. The reader does
not need to be told what to feel about it.

**The three Massey numbers travel together or not at all**: designed for 475-year,
achieves 150–240-year, new lifeline standard 2475-year. A single one of them out of
context is either alarming or reassuring and neither is accurate.

**Guards that must travel with numbers.**

- Every displacement figure carries its return period, its remediation state **and its
  location**. Queensborough's 150 mm is *after* remediation; without it, collapse. Oak
  Street's 100 mm is *after* a later densification scheme the paper does not say was
  built, and is tied to "design level earthquakes" rather than a return period. Golden Ears has two 2475-year figures — ~0.5 m at the river bank and
  ~0.3 m at the main piers after densification — which are not in conflict, but will read
  as conflicting if either qualifier is dropped.
- Golden Ears is 475/1000/2475, now confirmed by three independent [A] sources. Never
  write 975 for this bridge.
- Port Mann carries no number. If a draft acquires one, it came from somewhere that was
  not read. Say the absence in its true shape: three published papers on this bridge's
  seismic design, none of them free.
- **Every Mission Bridge figure carries its geography.** Mission connects Mission to
  Abbotsford, ~80 km east of Vancouver; it is a Fraser River crossing on the provincial
  disaster recovery network, not a Metro Vancouver commuter crossing. It must never appear
  in a sentence about Richmond or Delta.
- The North Arm Bridge's 100/475-year criteria are stated as *the project's own criteria
  as reported by its designer in 2008*, dated. No "would fail", no "under-designed", no
  comparison to CSA S6-14. The sourced statement is that project-specific criteria differ
  between owners and structures, and that Golden Ears and the North Arm Bridge are two
  published examples from the same decade.
- The 2023 North Shore review is about ship strikes, not earthquakes.

**The Massey contradiction is the site's best worked example of its own evidence rules,
and the rule it teaches is in `../CONVENTIONS.md`, not on a page.** Two official government
statements, 2011 and 2016, say the tunnel was seismically retrofitted. The province's own
2019 engineering memo says the ground-improvement stage was cancelled and the intended
level of safety was not achieved. The rule that resolves it — a press release is [A] for
its own existence, not for an engineering fact its own engineers contradict — is worth
more to the project than the finding.

**The audited table of blanks stays.** Filled rows beside blank ones make the point: the
assessments exist, they were recent, and with these exceptions they were not published.
The honest line is *assessed, not published*.

**Use the December 2015 event where a design standard would be reached for.** A magnitude
4.8 earthquake 52 km down stopped two SkyTrain lines for about eighty minutes on
intrusion alarms, with no damage. It is dated, checkable, and tells a reader more about
what a stoppage looks like than any return period. It must not be presented as a forecast
for a major earthquake.

**The 24-hour inspection line is the answer to "can I drive over it the next morning".**
The province writes that approximately 24 hours may be needed to complete a
post-earthquake inspection of a bridge. A bridge that survives is still not a bridge you
can use immediately, and that comes from the Ministry, not from us.

**Lever.** The reader cannot retrofit a bridge. What they can do is plan on the crossing
they use being closed or inspected rather than open, and know which side of the water
they need to be on. Route out of this page to `../mobility.md`.

**Do not write** that any crossing "will collapse", or that the region will be "cut off".
Neither is sourced. What is sourced is weeks to months of reduced capacity [PEIRS]
**[A]**, an inspection backlog the province plans for [MOTI-SRDC-05] [BCSIMS-22] **[A]**,
and one tunnel with a published shortfall and a 2031 fix. [GMC-TUNNEL-19] [MOTI-MASSEY]
**[A]**

**Link rot.** The two most valuable peer-reviewed sources here, [CAEE-10YR] and
[KSB-2010], sit on a domain that is mid-rebuild and already 404s at directory level.
Archive both before launch.
