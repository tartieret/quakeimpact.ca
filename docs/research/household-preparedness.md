**Status.** **Nobody has measured what the guidance asks for.** `preparedness.md` sets
out what households are told to hold — four litres per person per day and at least two
weeks of food and water in the earthquake guide. [PREPAREDBC] **Not one published study
measures days of food or litres of water held per household in the Lower Mainland.**
What is measured instead is whether a household says it owns a kit, whether it says it
has a plan, or whether it feels ready — all much weaker proxies, and the two that are
regionally representative both put kit ownership well under half.

So the site may say that most households here have not put together an emergency kit at
all. It may not say what share could last two weeks, because nobody knows.

Not a system, so no band. This subject supplies the home page's opening claim and
qualifies the lever every system page ends with.

## Findings

### Kit ownership in Metro Vancouver is under half, and it fell between 2019 and 2021

Research Co. polled 800 British Columbians online between 31 July and 3 August 2021,
weighted to census figures for age, gender and region, with a stated margin of error of
±3.5 percentage points, 19 times out of 20. **39 per cent of Metro Vancouver respondents
said they had purchased or prepared an emergency kit**; 38 per cent province-wide, down
eight points from the same firm's May 2019 survey. Regionally: Vancouver Island 45 per
cent, Fraser Valley 41, Metro Vancouver 39, Southern BC 30, Northern BC 29.
[RESEARCHCO-PREP-21] **[A]**

The same poll found 28 per cent with a plan covering how to contact family or friends,
down eleven points, and 22 per cent with an agreed meeting place, down thirteen.
[RESEARCHCO-PREP-21] **[A]**

This is the most defensible regional figure in the public record: representative,
weighted, and broken out for Metro Vancouver. It is also five years old, and a kit is
not a fortnight of supplies.

### British Columbia leads the country on kit ownership, which is a statement about the country

Statistics Canada's *Survey of Emergency Preparedness and Resilience in Canada* ran
January to June 2014 and remains the only nationally representative instrument on the
subject. **55 per cent of British Columbians lived in a household with an emergency
supply kit, the highest of any province**, alongside 71 per cent with an exit plan and
53 per cent in households scoring moderately high or high on a count of planning
activities — each the highest provincial figure. [STATCAN-SEPR-14] **[A]**

The survey is designed to support estimates for communities above 50,000 people, so
Surrey, Burnaby, Richmond and their peers are extractable; community-level fact sheets
followed in 2016. [STATCAN-SEPR-14] **[A]** The data is twelve years old and predates
both the 2021 flood and the current guidance.

### Having supplies and being able to last are different populations

PreparedBC's own December 2017 survey of 1,206 respondents found **54 per cent had done
some form of planning and 13 per cent described their plan as complete**, meaning a
written plan together with the supplies to execute it. A majority reported emergency
supplies of some nature — water, food or a flashlight. [PREPAREDBC-SURVEY-17] **[B]**

The distance between 54 and 13 is the useful part, and it is the province measuring its
own audience. "Has something in a cupboard" and "could get through a week" are not the
same population, and every kit-ownership figure above is the first of the two.

### Vancouver's own 2025 survey asks a broader question and cannot be read as a supply figure

UBC's Disaster Resilience Research Network ran a mixed-methods study with the City of
Vancouver Emergency Management Agency, part-funded by the Ministry of Emergency
Management and Climate Readiness: a City-administered preparedness module with 2,905
responses, a UBC-administered module on risk perception with 1,743, and six focus groups
totalling 50 people. **Just over 68 per cent reported having gathered at least some
emergency supplies or set money aside for an emergency.**
[UBC-PREP-25] **[A]**

That 68 per cent combines supplies with savings, which are not the same thing, and
neither implies three days of water. **The authors state that the results represent only
those who chose to participate and should not be read as representative of the city's
population.** [UBC-PREP-25] **[A]** It is City of Vancouver only, not Metro Vancouver.

Its other findings bear on why people are not prepared rather than on how many are: 78.8
per cent held homeowner, condo or tenant insurance; 29.9 per cent named lack of
accessible information as their biggest barrier, rising to roughly 40 per cent among
non-English speakers; only 15 per cent reported access to community-based support such
as a local hub or hands-on training; renters were less confident than owners about their
home's safety and their ability to return to it. Earthquakes and wildfire smoke were the
hazards named most often. [UBC-PREP-25] **[A]** The survey is to be repeated in 2027.

### The same two groups come last in every study that looks

A 2020 BCIT environmental-health study recruited Lower Mainland residents through
Facebook and Reddit groups tied to local geographies and found **41 per cent with an
emergency kit at home**, with language (p=0.025) and occupancy status (p=0.048)
significantly associated with preparedness: respondents whose first language was not
English, and renters, were less prepared. [BCIT-PREP-20] **[B]** It is a small
self-selected convenience sample and the percentage is directional at best; it is worth
recording because it is the only peer-reviewed study framed on the Lower Mainland, and
because its two significant associations are the same two the Vancouver and provincial
work finds independently.

### One widely repeated figure that is not a research finding

An Ipsos Reid survey for St John Ambulance BC and Yukon is often quoted as 34 per cent
of British Columbians believing they were prepared against 7 per cent who actually were.
The definition behind the 7 per cent and the method behind both are not documented in
the public coverage. [SJA-IPSOS-17] **[B]** **It is an advocacy figure and the site does
not use it.** Recorded here so that nobody re-finds it and takes it for a measurement.

### Figures that share a unit and cannot be compared

Every number above is a percentage of households or of individuals, and no two of them
measure the same thing. They differ in geography (province, city, Lower Mainland,
national with a provincial breakout), in sampling (weighted panel, national household
survey, self-selected social media recruitment, self-selected municipal outreach), in
definition ("has a kit", "has supplies or money set aside", "plan is complete", "is
prepared" against an undisclosed rubric) and in date, from 2014 to 2025.

**A trend line drawn across them would be an artefact of method, not a change in
behaviour.** The one within-instrument comparison that is legitimate is Research Co.'s
own 2019-to-2021 fall of eight points, because the firm asked the same question the same
way. [RESEARCHCO-PREP-21] **[A]**

### What the numbers should be measured against

PreparedBC's earthquake guide asks for four litres per person per day and at least two
weeks of food and water; its general kit page and its earthquake hazard page give
shorter figures, and the six published provincial and municipal answers are set out in
`preparedness.md`. [PREPAREDBC] **[A]** Restoration in the Lower Mainland is measured in
weeks to months for several systems, not in 72 hours — `impact-bands.md` carries the
assignment and the sources.

## What is not established

- **What share of Lower Mainland households could meet even the three-day water
  figure.** No published study measures held supply in days or litres. This is the gap,
  and it is the reason the site states kit ownership rather than sufficiency. **[?]**
- **Whether kit ownership has moved since 2021.** Research Co. has not repeated the
  question in a published release since, and the UBC/VEMA instrument asks something
  different. The 2027 repeat of the Vancouver survey is the next dated opportunity.
  **[?]**
- **A Metro Vancouver figure later than 2021 that is representative.** The only
  post-2021 work found is city-scoped and self-selected. **[?]**

**The answerable one.** SEPR's microdata carries variables beyond the published tables
and is reachable through the Research Data Centre network, which is the route to a
defensible Lower Mainland number rather than a press-release figure.
[STATCAN-SEPR-14] It would still be 2014 data.

## For the page

**The claim the home page rests on:** most households in this region have not put
together an emergency kit of any size. [RESEARCHCO-PREP-21] It is stated without a
percentage on purpose — the figure is five years old, and quoting one number invites the
reader to treat a poll of 800 as a census.

**The guard that governs every use of these figures:** kit ownership is not supply
sufficiency. Nothing on the site may use any of these percentages to say what share of
households could last two weeks, three days, or any other period. Where the question
comes up, the honest sentence is that nobody has measured it.

**The two groups that come last are the ones the site should keep in view** — renters
and households whose first language is not English — because three separate studies find
them independently, and because the barrier most often named is lack of accessible
information, which is the thing a public-information site can actually act on.
[UBC-PREP-25] [BCIT-PREP-20]

**Do not put these percentages in one chart or one table.** See the comparability
section above; the table would be the artefact, not the finding.
