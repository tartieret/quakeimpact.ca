## Status

The evidence now supports this subject, and it supports a different question from the one
it was written to answer. British Columbia's own planning documents state that mass
evacuation out of the impact area **will not happen and is not planned for**: public
messaging will emphasise sheltering within the region, and damage to routes plus the
prioritisation of essential personnel make mass evacuation impossible. Both statements
are **[A]**, from the province. That is a stronger, better-sourced and more useful framing
than asking who could get out. The supporting technical argument — that the approaches
fail before the spans — now has two Lower Mainland crossings behind it, and the
functional-retrofit finding in `systems/transportation.md` is firmer than anything this
subject previously had.

**Recommendation: reverse the demotion.** See "For the page".

## Findings

### The geography, which is a map fact and not a seismic claim

Vancouver is connected eastward by land through Burnaby and New Westminster. Richmond and
Delta are reached only by bridges and a tunnel. The North Shore has two vehicle crossings.

These are map facts. They carry no source key because they are not claims about
earthquakes, and they must be kept visually and typographically separate from the seismic
findings that sit on top of them. The previous draft was right that this distinction is
what keeps the section honest.

### The province's plan is that people do not leave

From the Provincial Earthquake Immediate Response Strategy's planning assumptions,
verbatim:

> "**Spontaneous mass evacuation out of the impact area will not take place:** … public
> messaging will emphasize **sheltering within the region** … Critical casualties,
> essential response personnel, those in imminent danger, and stranded travellers will be
> re-located as part of the immediate response." [PEIRS] **[A]**

From the province's Disaster and Climate Risk and Resilience Assessment, on its Cascadia
M9.0 scenario:

> "**Damage to transportation routes and the prioritization of essential personnel and
> supplies make mass evacuation impossible, and the public is directed to shelter in
> place.**" [DCRRA-2025] **[A]**

Two more PEIRS assumptions bear directly on the same question:

- "**Areas will be isolated:** Large parts of the impacted and surrounding area will be
  inaccessible by road due to earthquake-induced landslides, liquefaction, and other
  secondary impacts such as bridge collapses, and will be further isolated due to damage
  to airports and marine ports." [PEIRS] **[A]**
- "**Major transportation routes and nodes will be impacted:** Road, rail, air, and marine
  transportation will be disrupted, and **existing supply chains will be inoperable**."
  [PEIRS] **[A]**

And the scenario-level expectation: routes "damaged or only partially functional and
operating at a much-reduced capacity for an extended period (**weeks to months**)"; "the
rail network in the impact area may be largely unusable during the immediate response
phase". [PEIRS] **[A]**

**This changes the subject's premise.** The interesting question is not whether a reader
could drive out. It is that the province has planned on the assumption that they will not
try, that they should not try, and that the routes will be used for something else.

### Disaster Response Routes are not evacuation routes

- A network of pre-identified provincial and municipal roads was designated in **1995** in
  Metro Vancouver and Greater Victoria, to move emergency responders and supplies.
  [PITTMDW-DRR] **[A]**
- Routes are open to the public until a state of emergency is declared, then activated
  and controlled. Authorised users must carry government-issued photo identification *and*
  employment identification. [DRR-GOVPAGE] **[A]**
- **The official instruction to the public is to exit the route as soon as possible** to
  make way for emergency responders. [DRR-GOVPAGE] **[A]**
- Municipal mapping shows the network is explicitly multi-modal: the regional map legend
  distinguishes provincial from municipal routes and also marks muster points and large,
  medium and small vessel marine points. [DELTA-DRR-MAP] **[A]**
- The province's own backgrounder does not oversell them: "**Like any infrastructure,
  these routes are susceptible to hazards and could potentially be negatively impacted by
  the emergency event.**" [PITTMDW-DRR] **[A]**

**Presenting these as evacuation routes would teach the opposite of the correct
behaviour.** Explaining the signs is a genuine preparedness item and costs the reader
nothing. This is the site's clearest case of the rule against presenting
emergency-responder infrastructure as public infrastructure.

The province's route-planning guidance says something about the region that needs no
commentary. Under considerations for selecting roads and bridges for Disaster Response
Routes: "**Consider avoiding steep grades, circuitous routing, bridges, tunnels,
overpasses and underpasses.**" [DRT-PLANNING-18] **[A]** Seismic retrofit status is listed
in the same guide as an explicit vulnerability criterion for route selection, alongside
soil stability and proximity to gas and water utilities. [DRT-PLANNING-18] **[A]**

**A doctrine change the site must not describe wrongly.** The 1995 framework was
superseded in June 2018. "**DRRs are not designated pre-event. DRRs are determined at the
time of the event based on the needs of response and recovery and available options. DRRs
may or may not coincide with critical routes.**" What is designated in advance is now
called a **critical route**. [DRT-PRIMER-18] **[A]** The public-behaviour advice survives
unchanged; the description of a fixed pre-mapped network does not.

### Route recovery is planned, and part of the plan is withheld

This subject was previously listed as having no published route-recovery planning. Two
documents contradict that.

**Provincial.** The Disaster Response Transportation Primer's recovery section: "**The
identified critical routes are used as a reference to prioritize the restoration of
routes for regional mobility for all traffic.**" Before a route can be activated, five
things must be determined, including "**Assess the condition, capacity and serviceability
of transportation routes to determine the most viable route**". And: "**Emergency
responders should only use damaged routes after safety assessments have deemed the
damaged routes safe for emergency use.**" [DRT-PRIMER-18] **[A]**

**Regional and operational.** Metro Vancouver's *Joint Municipal Regional Disaster Debris
Management Operational Plan*, version 2.0, October 2017, 165 pages, sets an explicit
clearing-priority order in §2.5:

> "**Lifelines**: Routes essential to life safety transportation, e.g. evacuation routes.
> **Critical Infrastructure**, e.g. hospitals, ambulance halls, police stations, EOCs,
> telecommunication sites, water sanitation sites, power generation and transmission
> sites. **Clear major freeways and arterial routes.** Clear areas necessary for movement
> of goods and services and/or economic restoration. Clear minor arterial routes. Clear
> local routes." [MV-DEBRIS-17] **[A]**

And, memorably, "**Local Authorities may choose to use their snow removal routes as a
base**". [MV-DEBRIS-17] **[A]** Activation thresholds run local at 500–1,000 tonnes,
sub-regional at 1,001–8,000, and regional above 8,000 tonnes, with "significant
earthquake" given as the regional-scale example. These are activation thresholds, **not** a
scenario debris estimate — the plan directs authorities to run their own modelling.
[MV-DEBRIS-17] **[A]**

**Some of this material is deliberately not published.** The regional emergency-management
partnership states: "**Due to the sensitive nature of some of our work, some materials are
only available upon request.**" [IPREM-RES] **[A]**

That is a different and fairer fact about the world than "not found". A framework exists,
prioritisation orders are published, and part of the detail is withheld by choice. What
does **not** exist in public is any forecast of how long a named corridor would be shut.
The site must draw that line explicitly rather than blurring framework, design intent and
forecast together.

### The City says it to residents: the trains stop until the assessments are done

The City of Vancouver's Hazard & Risk Explorer, in its household planning advice: "**Plan
alternate ways to get home considering roads may be blocked and trains will not be running
until damage assessments are complete.**" [COV-EXPLORER-25] **[A]** ArcGIS item last modified
17 April 2025.

**This is the inspection bottleneck, from a municipal source and in plain language.** That
restoration is gated on assessment rather than on repair is already established in this project
from two other directions: the Ministry's own seismic retrofit criteria allow "approximately 24
hours … to complete a post-earthquake inspection" [MOTI-SRDC-05] **[A]**, and Scawthorn finds
mutual aid across the Fraser blocked initially "due to the need to inspect for damage"
[SCAWTHORN-2020] **[A]**. See `systems/transportation.md` and `buildings.md`; the mechanism is
cross-referenced, not restated here.

**What the City's sentence adds is threefold.** It applies the same gate to **rail transit**
rather than to bridges or fire response; it is the version a Vancouver resident is actually
given; and it is the City that would run the response saying it, rather than a ministry
standard or a consultant's report.

**Guard.** The sentence names no duration, and the City publishes none. It establishes a
sequence — assessment before service — and nothing whatever about how long the assessment
takes. Do not attach the Ministry's 24-hour bridge-inspection allowance to it: that is a design
criterion for a highway structure, not a transit restoration time, and the two are not the same
quantity.

### How long a severed BC corridor stays severed — rainfall, not earthquake

**Every figure in this block is from the November 2021 atmospheric river. None of it is
earthquake-triggered.** Label it that way every time it appears. With that caveat it is
the best available evidence of the duration of corridor severance in this province.

- **Highway 5, the Coquihalla.** Closed 14 November 2021. "More than 20 sites along 130
  kilometres … including **seven bridges where spans completely collapsed or were heavily
  damaged**." Reopened to commercial traffic in 35 days on 20 December 2021 and to all
  traffic on 19 January 2022 — about **66 days**. Four lanes restored December 2022; the
  last permanent bridge October 2023 — about **23 months to permanent repair**.
  [HWY5-RECOV] **[A]**
- **Highway 1, Fraser Canyon.** Closed 14 November 2021; **18 sites** between Hope and
  Spences Bridge; reopened 14 and 24 January 2022 on two segments — **61 and 71 days**.
  Permanent repairs ran to 2024 and 2025 with one culvert anticipated in 2026 —
  **roughly five years to full restoration**. No separate reopening date is published for
  Boston Bar–Hope. [HWY1-RECOV] **[A]**
- **Highway 8, Nicola Valley — the worst case.** 25 sites, more than 13 km of rebuilt
  roadway, two new bridges; reopened to the public 9 November 2022, about **12 months to
  any reopening**; two-lane traffic restored only in December 2025. [HWY8-RECOV] **[A]**
- **The whole event.** "The economic impact was estimated at **$5-7 billion**, and it
  severely affected Canadian National (CN) Railway and Canadian Pacific (CP) Railway,
  **cutting off main access routes to B.C. and Canada.**" [BC-FLOOD-STRAT] **[A]**
- Peer-reviewed corroboration describes the ground failures as "severing land connectivity
  between Metro Vancouver and the rest of Canada", covering the Fraser Canyon and the
  Coquihalla Valley. [SEPULVEDA-23] **[A]** In full: Sepúlveda, S.A., Ward, B.C., Cosman,
  S.B. & Jacobs, R. (2023), "Preliminary investigations of ground failures triggered
  during the mid-November 2021 atmospheric river event along the southwestern British
  Columbia highway corridors", *Canadian Geotechnical Journal* **60(4), 580–586**, DOI
  `10.1139/cgj-2022-0093`. The print year is 2023; indexes showing 2022 are giving the
  online-first date. **The third author is with the BC Ministry of Transportation and
  Infrastructure** — this is peer-reviewed *and* co-authored by the road authority, which
  is worth saying wherever the paper is used. Read as abstract and record only; the full
  text returned 403. A secondary summary attributing "at least 20 debris flows affecting
  major highways" to that paper is **[B] unverified — do not cite that number.**

**Do not state a Highway 99 or Highway 3 full-reopening date.** Neither was sourced.

### The approaches-not-spans argument now has evidence

The technical claim this subject rests on belongs in `systems/transportation.md` and is
cross-referenced, not restated. In summary: Queensborough's approach bents S3–S8 were
calculated at 150 mm differential displacement after soil remediation, and without that
remediation the predicted displacements "were considered large enough to cause a collapse
of the structure" [QB-MOTH-95] **[A]**; Golden Ears calculated ~0.5 m of river-bank
ground displacement at the 2475-year event [GEB-CGS-08] **[A]**; and lateral spreading
toward the Fraser and Deas Slough is described directly at the Massey crossing site
[GMC-BRIDGE-19] **[A]**.

A third crossing now supplies the only complete public loop in the evidence base — a
stated objective, quantified approach settlements, a predicted abutment flow slide, a
designed mitigation and a record that it was built. The **Mission Bridge** was retrofitted
to prevent collapse under a 475-year earthquake, with post-earthquake access and repairable
damage "considered desirable but not required"; settlements of up to 200 mm were predicted
at the south approach piers, and post-earthquake abutment factors of safety were 1.2 north
and **0.70 south — a predicted flow slide**. Horseshoe toe berms were designed and
constructed at both abutments. [MISSION-CGS-18] **[A]** *Geography, which travels with
every Mission figure: Mission connects the District of Mission to Abbotsford, roughly 80 km
east of Vancouver. It is a Fraser River crossing on the provincial disaster recovery
network, **not** a Metro Vancouver commuter crossing, and it must not drift into a sentence
about Richmond or Delta.* Detail in `systems/transportation.md`.

Alongside it sits the finding that reframes the whole subject: the province designates
routes that must stay open for emergency vehicles and states in the same document that it
is not currently retrofitting the bridges on them to remain in service. [MOTI-SRDC-05]
**[A]**

## What is not established

- **Any earthquake-triggered landslide susceptibility mapping for the Sea-to-Sky corridor
  (Highway 99) or the Fraser Canyon (Highway 1 / Highway 5).** Searched again in September
  2026, and the absence can now be stated more firmly. Channels searched: **Google
  Scholar**, with the query `earthquake-induced landslide susceptibility "Sea to Sky" OR
  "Highway 99" OR "Fraser Canyon" British Columbia seismic` (about 46 results, first two
  pages reviewed); **OpenAlex**, by title and by author; and **Crossref**, by bibliographic
  query. Nothing seismically conditioned and corridor-specific came back.

  What did come back, and why none of it closes the gap. A **landslide inventory** of the
  lower Sea-to-Sky corridor, whose triggers are rainfall, snowmelt, fluvial processes and
  construction, not earthquakes — the best corridor source covers 110 km, "more than 18%
  of Canada's total landslide related deaths (>600)" and 154 events from 1855 to 2007,
  with 54% climate and 6% construction triggers, and was grep-verified to contain **no
  mention of "earthquake" or "seismic"**. [S2S-BLAIS-08] **[A]** **National guidance**
  noting that susceptibility maps can be adapted to earthquakes as well as rainstorms — a
  statement of method, not a finding. A **1991 UBC thesis** examining the influence of
  seismic loading among the possible triggers of the single 1965 Hope Slide — a
  back-analysis of one event, not corridor susceptibility. **[B]** And rainfall-triggered
  susceptibility mapping in the peer-reviewed literature, likewise not seismically
  conditioned. [S2S-BRAYSHAW-12] **[B]**, paywalled and unverified. Every seismic landslide
  product located covers **western Metro Vancouver**, which stops short of Hope, or
  **Vancouver Island**. [MVSMMP] **[A]**

  **The caveat that limits the claim.** This was a literature search, not a sweep of
  provincial grey literature: web search was unavailable in the September 2026 pass, so
  MoTI technical circulars, GeoBC holdings and consultant reports were not re-swept, and
  the Ministry's geotechnical standards pages and its technical circulars index both
  return 404, so the circulars could not even be enumerated. State the finding as **"no
  published or mapped product was located"**, not "none exists".

  **Do not present Sea-to-Sky rockfall susceptibility as an earthquake finding.** It is a
  standing hazard an earthquake would plausibly aggravate; say exactly that or say
  nothing.
- **The Ministry excludes landslides from its bridge seismic criteria by its own scope
  statement**: "This document does not address other potential risks, such as
  **landslides** or tsunamis." [MOTI-SRDC-05] **[A]** The two hazards are managed in
  separate programs. That is a clean sourced explanation of why the mapping gap exists.
- **Howe Sound landslide-generated waves — closed, and the answer is deflationary.** "**A
  landslide into Howe Sound large enough to produce displacement waves that might cause
  damage on Bowen Island is unlikely.**" Federal mapping "concluded that none of the
  landslides that entered Howe Sound were large enough to produce a significant tsunami",
  and "no present instability of rock slopes bordering Howe Sound has been reported."
  [BOWEN-TSU-19] **[A]** The earlier North Shore assessment agrees: the probability is
  "very low, although not zero", and the waterfronts of North and West Vancouver are not
  at risk from such a wave. [NSEMO-TSU-05] **[A]** This is no longer an open question; it
  is a finding, and the finding is reassuring.
- **A published reopening-time estimate for any Lower Mainland corridor after an
  earthquake.** Not located, and not believed to be public. Distinguish carefully from the
  published recovery *framework* [DRT-PRIMER-18] [MV-DEBRIS-17] **[A]** and from published
  *design intent* — "restorable within one month" [MOTI-S6-SEC6] **[A]** and "approximately
  24 hours may be needed to complete a post-earthquake inspection" [MOTI-SRDC-05] **[A]**.
- **Which named crossings sit on the critical-route network.** The policy linkage is proven
  twice; the membership list is not published. Municipal maps located are rasters and the
  City of Vancouver regional map PDF returned HTTP 403 to both automated fetch and direct
  download in September 2026 — **verify or remove that link before launch.** Searched
  within budget only.
- **Whether any BC provincial review of the November 2021 event exists.** The search was
  never executed. Unresolved, **not** disproven. The well-known 2018 provincial review
  covers the **2017** season, not 2021; do not confuse them.
- **Unretrieved corridor sources:** GSC Open File 5741, *Historical accounts of landslides
  and flooding events along the Sea to Sky corridor, British Columbia, from 1855–2007*
  [GSC-OF-5741], the best located lead for corridor landslide history; and GSC Open File
  7616 on Howe Sound, whose conclusions are currently held at [A] only by secondary
  citation through [BOWEN-TSU-19]. [GSC-OF-7616]

## For the page

**Recommendation on priority: reverse the demotion, and change the question.**

The earlier judgement was that this was the most rhetorically satisfying part of the
project and the least defensible. That was correct when it was written and is no longer.
Three things changed:

1. **The premise is now sourced by the province itself, and it is more interesting than
   the old one.** "Mass evacuation will not take place" and "mass evacuation impossible …
   the public is directed to shelter in place" are two [A] provincial statements, from two
   separate documents, about the same thing. The section stops being an inference about
   geography and becomes a description of an actual government plan.
2. **The technical argument has evidence.** Approaches-not-spans is no longer reasoning
   from Kobe and Christchurch; it is Queensborough's own retrofit report and Golden Ears'
   own geotechnical paper.
3. **The functional-retrofit finding is stronger than anything this subject previously
   had**, and it belongs to this subject as much as to transportation: routes that must
   stay open, not retrofitted to stay open.

Build it after `systems/transportation.md`, because it depends on that file's findings —
but build it, and build it in the same MVP pass rather than deferring it. It should be
retitled around what the province plans, not around who could leave.

**The mechanism sentence.** British Columbia's emergency plans do not assume people will
drive out of the region after a major earthquake. They assume the opposite: that routes
will be damaged and reserved for response, that the public will be told to shelter where
they are, and that the roads will be cleared in a published order that puts life safety
and critical infrastructure ahead of getting anyone home.

**Keep the map facts visually separate from the seismic claims.** The peninsula-and-island
geography is a map; everything layered on it is sourced or marked. If a reader cannot tell
which is which, the section has failed its expert audit.

**The 2021 corridor figures carry a label every single time.** *Rainfall-triggered,
November 2021, not an earthquake.* Sixty-six days on the Coquihalla, sixty-one to
seventy-one days in the Fraser Canyon, twelve months on Highway 8 — these are the only
BC-specific corridor severance durations that exist, and they will be misread as
earthquake numbers the instant the label is dropped. They illustrate how long repair takes
in this terrain with this ministry and this contractor base. They do not forecast an
earthquake.

**"Deliberately not published" replaces "not found" wherever it is true.** The regional
debris plan's clearing order is public; some emergency-management material is withheld by
policy and available only on request. Saying that is more accurate and fairer than
implying nobody has done the work.

**Do not draw an evacuation map.** Ever. Explain what a Disaster Response Route sign means
and what to do when the reader is on one — get off it — and stop. This is the single most
likely way the site loses the trust of the emergency-management community.

**The Howe Sound answer is reassuring, and it ships anyway.** The style discipline cuts
both ways: a landslide-generated wave in Howe Sound large enough to do damage is unlikely,
and the site says so as plainly as it says the harder things.

**The going-home assumption now has a municipal sentence behind it.** Where the section needs a
local voice rather than a provincial one, the City's own advice says it: plan alternate ways to
get home, because roads may be blocked and trains will not be running until damage assessments
are complete. [COV-EXPLORER-25] **[A]** Use it for the sequence — assessment before service —
and attach no duration to it, because none is published.

**Lever.** The reader's action here is not a route; it is a location. Know which side of
the water you will be on during a working day, plan to stay there, and prepare the place
you will actually be rather than the place you would try to reach. That is what the
province's own assumption implies, and it is concrete enough to act on.

**Do not write** that the region would be "cut off", that people would be "trapped", or
that bridges "will fail". None is sourced. What is sourced is weeks to months of much
reduced capacity, a published clearing order, and a plan built on people staying put.
