## The province states the position in four sentences

From the province's Earthquake Immediate Response Strategy:

> "During a disaster, local and provincial government resources will be overwhelmed.
> In such an event, one's family and neighbours are likely to be the only available
> first responders. People living in an earthquake zone should aim to be
> self-sufficient for up to two weeks. Prepared neighbourhoods, families, and
> individuals will reduce pressure on overwhelmed government agencies and allow
> available resources to go where they are most needed." [PEIRS]

The last sentence is the one worth keeping. Preparing is not only about looking after
yourself. It is what frees the ambulance, the crew and the shelter place for the
person who has no other option, and that is the reason the province gives.

## Family and neighbours are the first responders, and the plan says so

The same strategy records that communities "informally self-activate" and "will work
together without receiving guidance from the Province". [PEIRS]

That makes the most valuable preparation a social one. Knowing which neighbours live
alone, who has medical needs, who has a wrench and who has a first aid certificate is
worth more in the first day than anything in a cupboard.

## Two weeks for households, against a support system designed for 72 hours

The province's Emergency Support Services program is the system that houses and feeds
people who have lost their homes. From the same page of the same document:

> "ESS is delivered by local emergency programs and is designed to provide up to 72
> hours of support … the ESS program is not designed for the scope and scale of
> services required after a catastrophic earthquake." [PEIRS]

This is not new, and it is not a single document's opinion. The province's emergency
management system said the same thing a decade earlier: in a catastrophic emergency
such as an earthquake, "ESS service delivery may not be able to manage the scope and
volume of needs". [BCEMS-2016]

Households are asked to cover two weeks. The support system behind them is built for
three days. The province states both, and states that the second is not sized for this
event.

## How long to prepare for depends on which document you open

Six official answers are in print, five of them provincial and one municipal. All are
current and all are quotable.

| Source | What it says |
| --- | --- |
| PreparedBC *Earthquake and Tsunami Preparedness Guide*, March 2024 [PREPAREDBC] | "at least two weeks", stated three times |
| PreparedBC general emergency kit page [PREPAREDBC-KIT] | "minimum three-day to one-week supply" |
| PreparedBC earthquake hazard page [PREPAREDBC-EQ] | "a minimum of 3 days", adding that one to two weeks is a good idea |
| The province's All-Hazard Plan, dated 2012 and still published [AHP-2012] | "self-sufficient for a minimum of 72 hours", with a caveat that recent events suggest a week or longer |
| The province's earthquake response strategy [PEIRS] | "self-sufficient for up to two weeks" |
| City of Vancouver [COV-EXPLORER-25] | "a minimum of 3 days, although your target should be a week to 2 weeks" |

Across the border, Washington State says two weeks everywhere, under a single named
campaign called Be 2 Weeks Ready. [WA-2WEEKS]

So the gap is not between countries. It is inside British Columbia's own advice. The
earthquake-specific guide, which is the document written for this hazard, says at
least two weeks. [PREPAREDBC]

The four Lower Mainland municipalities differ from each other too. Surrey says at
least 72 hours. [SURREY-PREP] The North Shore says up to two weeks. [NSEM-KIT] Richmond
publishes no household figure at all and points residents to the provincial guide.
[RICH-FR] Vancouver, which would run the response, prints the three-day arithmetic on
its own worksheet. [COV-WORKSHEET-26]

"Up to two weeks" and "at least two weeks" are the same number pointing in opposite
directions, which is why each one here is quoted in its own words.

## Water is the one number everyone agrees on

Four litres per person per day, for drinking and sanitation together. The province
publishes it. [PREPAREDBC] The City of Vancouver publishes the same rate.
[COV-EXPLORER-25]

What differs is the multiplier. The City's worksheet prints the sum against three
days. [COV-WORKSHEET-26] The provincial earthquake guide says at least two weeks.
[PREPAREDBC] For a household of four, that is the difference between 48 litres and at
least 224 litres. That arithmetic is ours, worked from two published rates, and
neither total is a figure either body publishes.

Two weeks of water is bulky. It is also the single most useful thing in a kit, because
the province's own Cascadia assessment says people are thirsty within 24 hours and
that trucking bulk water around the region is difficult for the first four to five
days. [DCRRA-2025] Stored water covers exactly that gap.

## Do not shut off your own gas, and two official sources disagree about it

FortisBC tells customers not to shut off their gas. If a customer shuts it off at the
meter, they must not turn it back on: only a registered gas contractor may restore
service, and restoration runs household by household, with a visit to each home to
relight every appliance. [FBC-EQ]

Surrey publishes the same advice with the consequence attached, which is the clearest
version anywhere in the region: "Leave natural gas service on unless officials tell
you to turn it off. If you go turn off the gas, the gas company has to reconnect it,
which may take weeks after a major emergency." [SURREY-PREP]

The City of Vancouver points the other way. Every City list of home preparations ends
with "Know where your gas, electric, and water shut-offs are located and know how to
turn them off." [COV-EXPLORER-25] A 2011 City worksheet, still linked from the City's
current guidance, goes further and says to keep a wrench close to the gas meter.
[COV-HHH-2011]

No City document tells anyone to shut the gas off after an earthquake. What it does is
tell everyone to know how. FortisBC tells customers not to, and says what it costs.
Both are official, both are current, and neither mentions the other.

## Securing what is in the room matters more than most people think

The province's guide covers fastening furniture, water heaters and heavy objects.
[PREPAREDBC] The City splits the same material into what a tenant can do without
drilling and what needs a landlord or a contractor, which is the more useful division
in a region where most people rent. [COV-EXPLORER-25]

Without drilling anything: heavy objects on lower shelves, framed pictures and mirrors
away from beds and seating, double-sided tape or velcro under smaller appliances, the
bed away from the window, and sturdy shoes under the bed. [COV-EXPLORER-25]

If you can fix things to the wall: tall furniture bracketed to studs, appliances
strapped, and the water heater tank secured to the wall. [COV-EXPLORER-25] The
provincial scenario's own description of the first minutes is mostly about objects and
glass, not collapse.

## Learn what a Disaster Response Route sign means before the day

Those routes are reserved for emergency responders once a state of emergency is
declared, and the official instruction to the public is to get off them.
[DRR-GOVPAGE] They are not evacuation routes and using them as such would get in the
way of the response.

No City of Vancouver public page explains this. The provincial page is the only place
it is set out. [DRR-GOVPAGE]

## What is being asked of households sits alongside work that is about half done

School seismic upgrading is the one public building program whose progress is
published. As of May 2026, 159 of 260 Metro Vancouver projects are complete and 101
remain. Province-wide the figure is 233 of 498. [SMP-PROGRESS]

## What you can do

In the order the province's own paragraph gives.

**Meet the neighbours.** Know who lives alone, who needs medication, who has mobility
limits, and who has tools. This costs nothing and it is what the plan is built on.

**Store water first.** Four litres per person per day [PREPAREDBC], as many days as
you can hold, refilled on a schedule you will actually keep.

**Then food that needs no cooking and no refrigeration**, and a manual can opener.
[PREPAREDBC]

**Then light, a power bank, warm layers and sturdy shoes**, kept where you will be
rather than only at home.

**Secure the heavy things in the rooms where people sleep.** Start with anything tall
next to a bed.

**Leave the gas on** unless an official tells you otherwise. [FBC-EQ] Know where the
shut-offs are anyway. [COV-EXPLORER-25]

**Agree an out-of-region contact.** Local networks saturate first.

**Build it over time if money is tight.** The province publishes advice on assembling
a kit on a budget, and the honest version of this page is that a partial kit is worth
much more than an intended one. [PREPAREDBC-BUDGET]
