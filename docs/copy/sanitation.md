## A toilet needs water to flush, and in an apartment tower there is nothing else to use

The water goes first. What happens to the toilet follows: flushing takes water somebody
has to spare, and after a major earthquake nobody has water to spare. Why the [water](/after/water/) stops, and for how
long, is set out there.

A house has a garden, a bucket, and somewhere to dig. A unit on the twentieth floor has
a bathroom, and when the water stops the bathroom stops with it.

The stored water a household is told to keep does not cover it, and the City of
Vancouver has done that arithmetic in public. PreparedBC tells residents to store four
litres per person per day, for drinking and sanitation. [PREPAREDBC] Vancouver's Rain
City Strategy sets that against ordinary use: it "falls short of normal daily water use
by more than 300 L per day, and provides the bare minimum for drinking and hygiene".
[COV-RAINCITY-19]

Four litres covers drinking and washing. There is nothing in it for the toilet, and it
is the City that says so.

## The only duration anyone has published for wastewater is many months

"Disruption to water and wastewater systems are expected for many months following the
event." [PEIRS] That is the Provincial Earthquake Immediate Response Strategy, the
province's own plan, and wastewater is named in it.

It covers the whole affected region rather than any one municipality, and it merges
water and wastewater into one sentence, so it does not say whether one returns before
the other. It also does not separate the treatment plants from the pipes, which is the
distinction the rest of this page turns on.

The province publishes shorter intervals for drinking water. They measure how long it
takes to truck bulk water to people, which is a different question from when a sewer
works again, and they sit with [water](/after/water/).

## Waste with nowhere to go is a health problem, and the province says so in its own words

The Provincial Earthquake Immediate Response Strategy, on what a disrupted waste
collection service does to people:

> "Disruptions to waste collection may result in biological hazards. Due to mass
> congregation in shelter spaces and possible inadequate disposal of garbage and
> biological waste, disease outbreaks are possible." [PEIRS]

*Possible* is the province's word and it is carrying weight. Not likely, not expected.

The sentence names its own condition: inadequate disposal. That is the step between a collection service that has stopped and a risk to health, and it is
the one link in the chain a household can act on.

It names waste collection, the garbage as well as the sewage, and a garbage truck needs
roads and fuel like everything else on a road. And it puts the risk in shelters, where
people are gathered together.

Christchurch shows what a failed collection system does to ordinary life, and it shows
nothing else. Chemical toilets were provided for about 30,000 residents and 1,141
portaloos were delivered. [CHCH-SEWER-STUFF] Temporary toilets stayed in use for weeks.
[CHCH-SEWER-NZH] Sewer repairs were projected to take more than a couple of years,
[CHCH-SEWER-STUFF] and a later council estimate put full network restoration at up to 30
years. [CHCH-RNZ] The last two figures are about rebuilding a network, not about how long
anyone used a portaloo. All of them describe Christchurch, and none of them says anything
about how long Richmond's or Delta's sewers would be out.

## A treatment plant built to survive an earthquake still needs the sewers that reach it

Metro Vancouver's biennial liquid waste report lists what it has built or designed to a
post-disaster standard, which means a structure the building code expects to keep
working after the earthquake rather than merely to stay standing: the Annacis Island
plant's Stage 5 expansion, the North Shore secondary treatment plant, the new Hollyburn
pump station, and the Sperling and Sapperton pump stations, which were under
construction with seismic upgrades. [MV-LWMP-BIENNIAL-19]

That is real work, and it is being done one plant and one pump station at a time.
Sewage reaches a treatment plant through pipes. A plant that survives still needs the
sewers that feed it, and the sewers are the part whose assessed condition is not public.

These are long programs with dates attached. Annacis Island serves about 1.25 million
people across 14 municipalities, and its remaining stage-gate approvals run from 2027 to
2030, with all components anticipated by 2035. [MV-ANNACIS] Iona Island was built in
1963, serves about 750,000 people, and is being rebuilt for secondary treatment with
seismic and flood-resilient design for its position on the delta. [MV-IONA]

Metro Vancouver's *Biennial Report 2017–2018, Integrated Liquid Waste and Resource
Management*, on a study it commissioned of its own sewers:

> "In 2018, the Liquid Waste Services Department completed a study evaluating seismic
> risks to Metro Vancouver's sewers, pump stations and wastewater treatment plants. Its
> findings are being used to inform seismic resiliency decisions in project designs and
> capital planning." [MV-LWMP-BIENNIAL-19]

> **Assessed in 2018, findings not released.** The region's own report names the
> document in its reference list: CH2M, *Seismic Risk Action Plan for Liquid Waste
> Services*, 2018, commissioned by Metro Vancouver. [MV-LWMP-BIENNIAL-19] Nothing from
> it has been published: no vulnerability ratings, no failure counts, and no expectation
> of how long the collection system would take to come back. [MV-CH2M-18] The sewers
> were in scope, and what the study found about them is the one thing a reader cannot
> read.

## Vancouver sets a seismic standard for new sewer pipe, which is not the same as knowing how the old pipe will do

The City of Vancouver's Engineering Design Manual carries a section headed Seismic
Design Standards inside its sanitary sewer part, with a parallel one for storm drainage.
[COV-EDM-26] It tells a designer to choose pipe material and replacement strategy from a
risk assessment of the ground: how susceptible the soil is to shaking, and its potential
for permanent ground deformation, which is ground that moves and does not move back.
[COV-EDM-26] It names the strategies too, among them routing mains outside liquefaction
zones, where saturated soil loses its strength and behaves like a liquid while the ground
shakes. [COV-EDM-26]

"Minimize seismic risk to sewage and drainage services" is objective 3.4 of the City's
Healthy Waters Plan. [COV-HWP-25] How the plan proposes to measure progress against it
is the honest part: a rating scale of 1 to 5 by expert judgement. [COV-HWP-25]

A standard for new pipe is not an assessment of old pipe. Nothing published says how the
network already under the streets would perform, and nothing says the replacement pipe
performs better or worse than what it replaces. Nobody has measured it.

> **No municipality has published an assessment of its own sewers.** Metro Vancouver
> owns the trunk sewers and the treatment plants. The pipe under a residential street
> belongs to the municipality, and no seismic vulnerability assessment of a municipal
> sewer network has been published for Vancouver, Delta, Surrey or New Westminster. The
> only duration published for this system anywhere in the region is the province's many
> months, covering water and wastewater together. [PEIRS]

## What you can do

The province expects water and wastewater disruption to last many months, and it names
inadequate disposal of waste as the step between a stopped collection service and a risk
to health. [PEIRS] That step is the one a household controls.

**Settle now how the household would contain human waste with no water to flush.** A
pail with a tight lid, heavy bags, and something dry to cover each use is the whole of
it. It costs almost nothing and takes almost no storage space, which is the objection
most preparedness advice runs into.

**Do not plan to flush with stored water.** The four litres per person per day PreparedBC
advises is for drinking and sanitation, [PREPAREDBC] and the City of Vancouver puts that
figure more than 300 litres a day below normal use. [COV-RAINCITY-19] Water put by for
drinking and washing does not stretch to the toilet as well.

Vancouver's advice to store water assumes a household with somewhere to put a bin of it.
[COV-RAINCITY-19] The toilet is the part of the problem that stored water does not
solve, which is why the pail is worth settling before the water stops rather than
after.
