## A pipe under a road and a pipe under a river are different repairs

Lucas Pitts, director of policy, planning and analysis at the Greater Vancouver Water
District, describing his own system:

> "It's one thing for a pipe under the Lougheed Highway to leak. We can go dig that
> out, we can throw a patch on it, we can be out of there in four hours. But for a
> crossing under the Fraser River, obviously, if that breaks, to fix that takes a very
> long time." [MV-WATER-22]

That is the mechanism for this whole page. The number of breaks matters less than
where they are.

## A magnitude 9.0 is modelled to cause 267 main failures, and about 60 of them at crossings

Metro Vancouver commissioned a seismic assessment of the regional water supply system,
completed in February 2022. Its findings reached the public through a heavily redacted
release obtained by Glacier Media, which reported them in April 2022. [MV-WATER-22]

Under a magnitude 9.0 megathrust, the study modelled:

- **267 water main failures** across the region and its 21 municipalities.
- **22 water mains** rated at high seismic vulnerability, running through the North
  Shore, Vancouver, Burnaby, New Westminster, the Tri-Cities, Maple Ridge, Richmond,
  Delta and Surrey.
- **About 60 breaks across the 71 water crossings**, including three to four in the
  hardest category, which is the Fraser River, the Pitt River, False Creek and Burrard
  Inlet.

[MV-WATER-22]

The two counts measure different things and both are real. 267 counts failures across
the whole network. 60 counts breaks at the crossings alone, which is a subset of the
267 falling on the hardest assets to reach.

The figures come from a review of 123 existing reports on reservoirs, pump stations
and pipelines rather than from a single model run. [MV-WATER-22] They are more than
eight and a half times the failures projected by the region's previous study in 1993.
[MV-WATER-22] The study is four years old.

## Nobody has published how long the water would take to come back

The obvious next question has a specific answer, and it is not a duration.

The two pieces of the study a restoration estimate would be built from were removed
from the public version under freedom-of-information exemptions: the number of leaks
and breaks per main, and the damage estimates for 21 water storage facilities.
[MV-WATER-22]

Metro Vancouver's own Drinking Water Management Plan 2026, which is the region's
governing drinking water document, contains no restoration estimate of any kind. It
names seismic risk as a core pressure, and the first action under its resilience
strategy is to "increase the seismic resilience of the water system by conducting
prioritized structural analysis to identify seismic vulnerabilities". [MV-DWMP-26]

In 2026, identifying the weak points is still work to be done rather than work
completed.

The plan also shows where responsibility divides. Metro Vancouver owns the
transmission mains; the member municipalities own the pipes in the street. The plan's
third action commits to coordinating with those municipalities "to identify points of
possible failure", which says in the region's own words that the joined-up picture
across that seam has not been assembled yet. [MV-DWMP-26]

> **Not the page's estimate.** A March 2018 case study by Agriculture and
> Agri-Food Canada found that drinking water disruption in the Vancouver area "could
> last several months". It is a federal document, never published, obtained under
> freedom of information and reported by the Globe and Mail in 2019. It predates the
> current tunnel program. Its more durable finding is the other half: at the time, the
> province had put its plans for supplying potable water on hold pending an update
> from Metro Vancouver. [GM-AAFC-19]

## The province gives a duration, and it covers sewers in the same sentence

"Disruption to water and wastewater systems are expected for many months following
the event." [PEIRS] That is the province's own statement, and it is qualitative and
region-wide rather than specific to any pipe.

There is a second and much shorter clock, and it is a different measurement. The
province's Cascadia assessment states that "people are thirsty within 24 hours", and
that "distributing bulk potable water across the affected region remains challenging
for the first four to five days". [DCRRA-2025]

That four to five days is about trucking water to people. The many months is about
the network being repaired. Merging them would suggest water comes back in a week.

## One municipality publishes numbers, and they do not transfer

New Westminster's potable water emergency response plan carries a damage and duration
table in its earthquake annex: 200 to 300 breaks in a 475-year event, at
"approximately 1-3 days per break". It gives "weeks to months" for the loss of the
regional supply through landslides. [CNW-PWERP-23]

Every part of that is narrower than it looks. It covers New Westminster's own
distribution system. Its scenarios are 1-in-100 and 1-in-475 year events, which are
much smaller than either scenario on this site. The one to three days is how long one
break takes to repair, not how long the city waits, because turning it into a
restoration time needs a crew count the plan does not give. And the weeks to months
attaches to the regional supply being cut by landslides, not to those 200 to 300
breaks.

It is here because it is the only published set of numbers of its kind in the region.

## Five deep tunnels are being built, and none of the current four is finished

Metro Vancouver is replacing its river and inlet crossings with deep bored tunnels
"designed to ensure delivery of drinking water in the event of a major earthquake".
[MV-ANNACIS-TUNNEL]

| Tunnel | Status |
| --- | --- |
| Port Mann | Completed 2017, the first of them [MV-TUNNELS] |
| Second Narrows | 30 m below the bottom of Burrard Inlet, about 1 km long; expected complete in 2028 [MV-SNT-PAGE] |
| Annacis | Started 2022, expected complete at the end of 2028 [MV-ANNACIS-TUNNEL] |
| Stanley Park | Under construction, replacing a 1930s main [MV-STANLEY-TUNNEL] |
| Cambie-Richmond | In design [MV-CAMBIE-FS] |

Metro Vancouver describes all of them the same way, as meeting current seismic
standards. The contrast worth holding is in the region's own words: five new deep
tunnels are being built so that water keeps flowing after a major earthquake, and the
crossings they replace are where the 267 failures fall.

Elsewhere the work runs later. The seismic phase of the Palisade Lake outlet works is
scheduled to start in the mid-2030s and finish in the early 2040s, timed so that
draining the lake does not hit summer supply. [MV-WAT-2025-09] As of 2023, four
reservoirs built before the 1990s still needed upgrading. [MV-RESERVOIR-23]

Metro Vancouver has identified this problem, measured it, and is spending heavily
against it. The program is not finished, and the dates are the point.

## What you can do

The province says people are thirsty within 24 hours, and that trucking bulk water
around the region is hard for the first four to five days. [DCRRA-2025] Stored water
at home covers exactly that gap, which is the clearest match between an action and a
sourced interval anywhere on this site.

**Store four litres per person per day.** That is the province's own figure, for
drinking and basic sanitation together. [PREPAREDBC] How many days to multiply it by
is the question BC's own guidance answers differently in different documents, and
[preparing](/prepare/) sets out what each of them says.

**Include pets, and include a way to treat water.** The provincial guide covers both.
[PREPAREDBC]

**Refill and rotate it.** Stored water that has been in a cupboard for six years is
the commonest failure of a household kit.
