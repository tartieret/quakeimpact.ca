## The province plans on people staying

From the planning assumptions in the province's Earthquake Immediate Response
Strategy:

> "Spontaneous mass evacuation out of the impact area will not take place: … public
> messaging will emphasize sheltering within the region … Critical casualties,
> essential response personnel, those in imminent danger, and stranded travellers will
> be re-located as part of the immediate response." [PEIRS]

From the province's risk and resilience assessment, on its Cascadia scenario:

> "Damage to transportation routes and the prioritization of essential personnel and
> supplies make mass evacuation impossible, and the public is directed to shelter in
> place." [DCRRA-2025]

Two documents, two levels of the same government, the same instruction. The useful
question is therefore not whether a person could drive out. It is that the plan
assumes they will not, and that the roads will be doing other work.

Two further assumptions from the same strategy sit underneath it. Large parts of the
area "will be inaccessible by road due to earthquake-induced landslides, liquefaction,
and other secondary impacts such as bridge collapses". And "Road, rail, air, and
marine transportation will be disrupted, and existing supply chains will be
inoperable." [PEIRS]

## The geography under all of this is simple, and the intuition about it is wrong

These are map facts and they carry no source, because they are not claims about
earthquakes.

**Vancouver is a peninsula, not an island.** It is bounded to the north by Burrard
Inlet and to the south by the North Arm of the Fraser, and it is connected by land
eastward through Burnaby and New Westminster. That land route is the city's real
lifeline.

**Richmond and Delta are the genuine islands.** Lulu Island and Sea Island are reached
only by bridges and a tunnel, whose approaches cross the same soft delta ground on both
banks.

**The North Shore has two vehicle crossings** and mountains behind it.

Everything layered on top of that geography, on this site, is sourced.

## Disaster Response Route signs mean stay off, not evacuate this way

Most people in the region have seen the signs. Very few have been told what they mean,
and the correct behaviour is the opposite of the instinctive one.

A network of provincial and municipal roads was designated in 1995 across Metro
Vancouver and Greater Victoria to move emergency responders and supplies.
[PITTMDW-DRR] The routes are open to everyone until a state of emergency is declared,
and then they are activated and controlled. Users must carry both
government-issued photo identification and employment identification. [DRR-GOVPAGE]

**The official instruction to the public is to get off the route as soon as
possible**, to make way for emergency responders. [DRR-GOVPAGE]

The province does not oversell them either: "Like any infrastructure, these routes are
susceptible to hazards and could potentially be negatively impacted by the emergency
event." [PITTMDW-DRR]

The doctrine changed in June 2018. Response routes are now chosen at the time of the
event rather than fixed in advance: "DRRs are not designated pre-event. DRRs are
determined at the time of the event based on the needs of response and recovery and
available options." What is designated in advance is now called a critical route.
[DRT-PRIMER-18] The advice to the public is unchanged.

One line in the province's route planning guidance describes this region without
needing any commentary. Among the considerations for choosing roads for response
routes: "Consider avoiding steep grades, circuitous routing, bridges, tunnels,
overpasses and underpasses." [DRT-PLANNING-18] Seismic retrofit status is listed in
the same guide as a reason to rule a route out.

## Roads are cleared in a published order, and getting people home is not near the top

Metro Vancouver's regional debris management plan sets the clearing priority:

> "Lifelines: Routes essential to life safety transportation, e.g. evacuation routes.
> Critical Infrastructure, e.g. hospitals, ambulance halls, police stations, EOCs,
> telecommunication sites, water sanitation sites, power generation and transmission
> sites. Clear major freeways and arterial routes. Clear areas necessary for movement
> of goods and services and/or economic restoration. Clear minor arterial routes. Clear
> local routes." [MV-DEBRIS-17]

Local roads are last, and the plan suggests a practical starting point that anyone
who has lived through a snowfall will recognise: "Local Authorities may choose to use
their snow removal routes as a base". [MV-DEBRIS-17]

Provincially, the same sequencing applies to reopening. Critical routes "are used as a
reference to prioritize the restoration of routes for regional mobility for all
traffic", and "Emergency responders should only use damaged routes after safety
assessments have deemed the damaged routes safe for emergency use." [DRT-PRIMER-18]

> **Not published.** There is no public forecast of how long any named Lower Mainland
> corridor would be closed. What is published is the framework for reopening them
> [DRT-PRIMER-18] [MV-DEBRIS-17], and design intent for new structures, such as half
> the lanes restorable within one month after the largest design earthquake
> [MOTI-S6-SEC6]. A framework is not a forecast, and neither is a design target. Some
> regional emergency-management material is withheld by choice: "Due to the sensitive
> nature of some of our work, some materials are only available upon request."
> [IPREM-RES]

## How long a severed corridor stays severed here, from rainfall rather than an earthquake

Every figure in this section comes from the atmospheric river of November 2021. None
of it was caused by an earthquake. With that said, it is the only measured evidence
of how long it takes to reopen a road in this terrain, with these contractors.

- **Highway 5, the Coquihalla.** Closed 14 November 2021, with more than 20 damaged
  sites along 130 km, including seven bridges where spans collapsed or were heavily
  damaged. Reopened to commercial traffic in 35 days and to all traffic after about 66
  days. The last permanent bridge was finished in October 2023, about 23 months later.
  [HWY5-RECOV]
- **Highway 1, the Fraser Canyon.** Closed the same day, 18 damaged sites between Hope
  and Spences Bridge, reopened after 61 and 71 days on two segments. Permanent repairs
  ran into 2025. [HWY1-RECOV]
- **Highway 8, the Nicola Valley.** 25 sites and more than 13 km of rebuilt road.
  Reopened to the public about 12 months later. [HWY8-RECOV]

The provincial flood strategy puts the economic impact of that event at $5 to $7
billion and records that it cut "main access routes to B.C. and Canada".
[BC-FLOOD-STRAT] Peer-reviewed work on the same event describes the ground failures as
"severing land connectivity between Metro Vancouver and the rest of Canada", and one of
its authors works for the road authority. [SEPULVEDA-23]

Those are repair durations for rainfall damage. They illustrate the pace of this kind
of work. They forecast nothing about an earthquake.

## A landslide wave in Howe Sound is assessed as unlikely

Not every answer on this site is a bad one. Federal mapping "concluded that none of
the landslides that entered Howe Sound were large enough to produce a significant
tsunami", and a landslide into the sound large enough to produce damaging waves on
Bowen Island is assessed as unlikely. [BOWEN-TSU-19] An earlier North Shore assessment
agrees, putting the probability as "very low, although not zero" and finding the North
and West Vancouver waterfronts not at risk from such a wave. [NSEMO-TSU-05]

## Nobody drives out on the first day

There is no fuel, the roads are blocked and cleared in an order that puts local
streets last [MV-DEBRIS-17], and everyone would be trying at once. The province
expects weeks to months of much reduced capacity on the routes that exist. [PEIRS]

Where people do leave a damaged region, they leave slowly rather than at once, worn
down by cold, absent services, closed schools and no work. How many people would leave
the Lower Mainland, and over what period, has not been published.

## What you can do

The action on this page is not a route. It is a location.

**Decide where you would rather be stuck, and be there.** Know which side of the water
you will be on during a working day, and plan to stay on that side.

**Prepare the place you will actually be**, not the place you would try to reach. For
most people that means the workplace deserves a small kit too.

**Plan a way home on foot, and expect transit to be stopped for inspection**, not
running. The City's own advice is to plan alternate ways home because trains will not
run until damage assessments are done. [COV-EXPLORER-25]

**Agree an out-of-region contact with your household.** Local phone networks saturate
first, and a single agreed person elsewhere is easier than everyone calling everyone.

**If you are on a Disaster Response Route when one is activated, get off it.** That is
the instruction, and it is the one piece of this page that changes what a person does
in the first hour.
